What did not hold up

This section lists the hypotheses that failed and the tests that gave no answer. A search for structure is only as informative as the structures that it rules out. The first five rows come from the pilot; the other six come from the main studies.

HypothesisEvidenceWhat we found
Languages are positions on a torus, and the maps between them are rotations, as in rotary position embeddings.exploratory (pilot)The maps are mostly an offset. The small rotations that remain do not commute (commutator 0.55 to 0.72, against 0.68 to 0.80 for random rotations) and spread over about 16 planes. The only one-dimensional "position" is the token count (|r| = 0.93 to 0.95), which comes from the pooling.
The failure of the maps to compose has a low-dimensional structure linked to script or family.exploratory (pilot)The failure was the artefact of per-language PCA (A first look). With one shared subspace, the maps compose.
A language centroid is the sum of a script vector and a family vector.exploratory (pilot)Leave-one-language-out prediction from script and family is worse than prediction by the mean (R² from −0.27 to −0.44).
Language offsets rotate into the unembedding subspace in the late layers.exploratory (pilot)The offsets are never more aligned with the unembedding than the sentence content is (ratio 0.90 to 1.28).
The pilot can test a family tree in 12 languages.simulationToo few languages: the power was 18% to 42% in simulation. The main study therefore uses 34 languages (power 80% to 86%).
The word-order axis is specifically object–verb order.pre-registeredNot supported. Adposition order explains the distances beyond object–verb order in all four analyses. The axis is the head-direction bundle.
At a code switch, the representation follows a Bayesian observer of the whole sequence.pre-registeredNot supported. A step fits better (held-out error 0.117 against 0.250 at layer 14).
The word-order direction changes word order in free generation (first study).pre-registeredNot evaluable. The inclusion rule excluded all 8 languages; no claim, positive or negative. A later pre-registered replication confirmed the effect for noun objects in German and Russian.
The word-order direction changes the order of noun objects in four new languages with flexible order (confirmatory H2).pre-registeredNot evaluable. At k = +2, only 27% of Polish and 37% of Croatian continuations stayed in the prompt language, so only 2 of the 4 languages had a defined slope (minimum 3). Dutch and Ukrainian changed as predicted (descriptive).
A push against a language's own order makes the model leave the language more often than a push toward it.exploratory, then pre-registered secondaryNot confirmed. The first study found it in 7 of 7 languages (p = 0.016, exploratory). The confirmatory study found it in 7 of 9 (p = 0.18).
The word-order direction reorders objects in French.exploratoryAn artefact of object type. None of 218 French noun objects precedes its verb in any condition; the share rises because noun objects disappear.
