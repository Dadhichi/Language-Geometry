Steering free generation

This section asks whether the word-order direction changes the order in which the model itself writes verbs and objects. This test is stronger than the preference test. The model must commit to an order, and each language has its own rules for when an object can come first.

The first pre-registered test could not be evaluated. Exploratory analysis of the same run shows that the direction reorders noun objects in Russian and German, at a strength that also degrades the text. A second, pre-registered test on fresh prompts confirms this in German and Russian. Its test of four new languages could not be evaluated.

Design of the first study (pre-registered)

  • Prompts. 150 FLORES+ devtest sentences in 8 languages: English, German, French, Russian, Hindi, Turkish, Japanese and Chinese. Each prompt is the first 40% of the words of a sentence (of the characters, for Chinese and Japanese). This gives 1,200 prompts with the same content in every language.
  • Generation. Greedy decoding of 40 new tokens, cut at the first newline.
  • Steering. h^{14}_t\leftarrow h^{14}_t+k\,v at every position. The direction v is one of three kinds: the held-out word-order direction of the prompt language, the Indo-European direction, or one of 24 random directions, all at the same norm.
  • Conditions. Unsteered; word-order direction at \pm k^\star and \pm k^\star/2; Indo-European direction at \pm k^\star; 24 random directions at \pm k^\star. These 55 conditions give 66,000 continuations.
  • Strength rule. We calibrated on the first 20 prompts per language with 3 random directions at k\in\{\pm0.5,\pm1,\pm2\}. The rule takes the largest k at which continuations stay in the prompt language at a rate of at least 80% of the unsteered rate. It chose k^\star=2 (88.8% against 98.8% unsteered).
Measurement

A language identifier [14], restricted to the 8 languages, labels each continuation. Only continuations in the prompt language count. This prevents a switch into another language from counting as a change of word order.

Stanza [12] parses each prompt with its continuation. For a continuation x, the set A(x) holds its counted pairs. A counted pair is a dependency with relation \texttt{obj} (any subtype) whose head has the part of speech VERB, with both words in the generated text. Then

n_{\text{OV}}(x)=\big|\{(v,o)\in A(x):\ \mathrm{pos}(o)<\mathrm{pos}(v)\}\big|,\qquad \rho_i(k v)=\frac{\sum_{x\in X_i(kv)}n_{\text{OV}}(x)}{\sum_{x\in X_i(kv)}\lvert A(x)\rvert},

where X_i(kv) holds the continuations of the prompts of language i, steered with kv, that the identifier assigns to language i. We call \rho_i the object-first share; \rho_i(0) is the unsteered share.

The effect of a direction v on language i is the slope of the object-first share in the strength, in share per unit of k:

b_i(v)=\frac{\rho_i(+k^\star v)-\rho_i(-k^\star v)}{2k^\star}.

The pre-registered claim was that the mean of b_i(\beta_{\text{OV}}) over the included languages is positive and exceeds the means of all 24 random directions (empirical p = 1/25 = 0.04), with z>2.33. A language was included if it had at least 20 counted pairs in every one of the 53 conditions that the test needs.

The pre-registered test could not be evaluated

No language met the inclusion rule. Most random directions at k=2 do little harm, but a few push a language almost completely out of itself. In 7 of the 8 languages, between 3 and 9 of the 48 random conditions left fewer than half of the continuations in the prompt language. Chinese had no such random condition, but the word-order direction at k = +2 left only 8% of its continuations in Chinese. Each such condition leaves too few pairs to count. Because the rule required all 53 conditions, it excluded every language.

The design was inconsistent. The strength rule limited the average damage of random directions, but the inclusion rule needed every direction to be harmless. The calibration saw 3 of the 24 directions on 20 prompts per language, too few to show this tail. By the rule that we committed to, this study makes no claim, positive or negative. The next three subsections are exploratory: we decided their analyses after we saw the data.

What the generations show (exploratory)

We decide inclusion separately for each direction. Language i enters the mean for direction v if the unsteered condition and both \pm k^\star v have at least 20 counted pairs:

\bar b(v)=\frac1{\lvert\mathcal L_v\rvert}\sum_{i\in\mathcal L_v}b_i(v),\qquad \mathcal L_v=\Big\{i:\ \min_{k\in\{0,\,+k^\star,\,-k^\star\}}\ \sum_{x\in X_i(kv)}\lvert A(x)\rvert\ \ge 20\Big\}.

For the word-order direction, \mathcal L_v contains five languages: English, French, German, Hindi and Russian. Japanese, Turkish and Chinese drop out. In each of them, the word-order direction, pushed against the language's order, makes the model leave the language. Only 40% of continuations stay in Japanese at k = −2, 55% in Turkish at k = −2, and 8% in Chinese at k = +2.

The pattern matches the preference test (). Each language moves only toward the order that it does not already prefer. Russian, which prefers verb-first, becomes more object-first under +k and does not change under −k (9% unsteered, 8% at −2). German and Hindi, which prefer object-first in these continuations, become more verb-first under −k and change little under +k.

Russian is the clearest case. Its grammar allows an object before the verb for emphasis or style, and the steered continuations remain fluent Russian. German shows a different effect of the same push. Steered toward verb-first, the model writes German with English verb placement, which German grammar does not allow in these clauses. In the examples, verbs are bold and objects are underlined.

Russian prompt Влага на ваших руках вступит в реакцию (The moisture on your hands will react)
unsteered … с кислотой, которая содержится в зубной пасте, и образует щелочь. (… with the acid in the toothpaste, and forms an alkali.)
k = +2 … и кожу разъест. (… and will corrode the skin; word for word "and the skin will-corrode".)

unsteered … модели» и «создавать новые институты, которые будут соответствовать новым реалиям». (… model" and "to create new institutions that will match the new realities".)
k = +2 …, устаревшей, непродуктивной» модели экономического развития, «новую, эффективную, продуктивную» модель экономического развития выбрать, призывал (…, outdated, unproductive" model of economic development, "a new, efficient, productive model of economic development to choose", he urged)

unsteered … der Lage sind, die Bewegungen des Benutzers zu erfassen und zu interpretieren. (… able to capture and interpret the user's movements; object before verb, as German requires.)
k = −2 … der Lage sind zu messen die Entfernung zwischen die beiden Sensoren und die Hand. (… able to measure the distance between the two sensors and the hand; English order, not grammatical in German.)

Averaged over the five included languages, the word-order direction gives \bar b(\beta_{\text{OV}})={+6.8} percentage points per unit of k. This is a change of 27 points in the object-first share between k = −2 and k = +2. The 24 random directions give +0.1 ± 0.7 points (largest +1.1), and the Indo-European direction gives +0.8. The word-order slope exceeds every random slope, with z = 10.0. It also exceeds each random direction when both use only the languages that both can use (24 of 24). A bootstrap over prompts puts its 95% interval at +4.3 to +8.6.

Per language, the word-order slope exceeds all random slopes in Russian (z = 5.9), French (4.1), Hindi (2.2) and German (1.9). In English it is near zero (z = 0.2). The French value does not reflect reordering, as the next subsection shows.

How much of this is reordering? (exploratory)

The object-first share is a ratio. It can change when no object moves, for example when continuations contain fewer verb-first objects. Three checks limit the interpretation.

Object type. Romance languages put unstressed object pronouns before the verb as a rule (French je le vois, "I see it"), and Russian often does. A push that produces more pronouns or fewer nouns raises the share without reordering. We parsed the five included languages again under the unsteered condition, the word-order direction at ±2 and all 48 random conditions. We split each counted pair by the part of speech of the object: noun (NOUN or PROPN), pronoun (PRON) or other. The new parse reproduces the original counts exactly for 94% to 97% of continuations, depending on the language.

Share of noun objects and of pronoun objects placed before their verb in the five included languages, at k = −2, unsteered and at k = +2. n is the number of counted pairs. Shares based on fewer than 10 pairs are not shown.

German and Russian reorder noun objects. In German, the share of noun objects before their verb falls from 72% to 14% under k = −2. German pronouns stay in front (96% and 92%). In Russian, the noun-object share rises from 1% to 36% under k = +2.

French does not reorder at all: none of its 218 noun objects precedes its verb in any of the three conditions. Its share rises under k = +2 because noun objects almost disappear (6 left, against 100 unsteered). This leaves mostly pronouns, which come before the verb. In Hindi, the share of noun objects before the verb falls from 92% to 53%, but on only 17 pairs. The rest of the Hindi change comes from objects that are neither nouns nor pronouns. These occur only when steered, and 19 of 20 come after the verb.

For noun objects alone, with the same inclusion per direction, English, German and Russian qualify. Their pooled slope is +8.6 points per unit of k. Random directions give +0.3 ± 1.0 (largest +2.8), so z = 8.2.

Text quality. With w_1,\dots,w_n the words of a continuation, its repetition is the share of word bigrams that repeat an earlier bigram:

\mathrm{rep}(x)=1-\frac{\big\lvert\{(w_j,w_{j+1})\}_{j<n}\big\rvert}{n-1}.

Averaged over the five languages, repetition is 4.6% unsteered, 7.5% under the word-order direction, 6.8% under the Indo-European direction and 6.1% under random directions. The mean of a single random condition ranges from 0% to 25%. The word-order push degrades text more than a typical random push of the same norm, and much less than the worst one.

Strength. At k = ±1 the slope is near zero in every language except Hindi. It is +15.7 points per unit of k in Hindi, and at most +1.5 in the others. Except in Hindi, the change appears only at the strength where random directions start to break language identity. The push that reorders is therefore also a push that causes damage.

Language identity is part of the direction (exploratory)

When the word-order direction makes the model leave a language, it does so for one sign of k only. For each language, the sign of k that pushes it away from its own majority order is against, and the other sign is toward.

The destination language varies. Japanese pushed toward verb-first mostly becomes Chinese, and French pushed toward object-first mostly becomes Japanese. But Chinese pushed toward object-first becomes English, so the destination is not simply a language with the target order. We estimated the direction as a difference between groups of languages, so it carries part of what distinguishes those languages as languages. In Japanese, Turkish and Chinese, whose order is fixed, the model more often leaves the language than breaks its order. German is the exception: its verb placement is also fixed by rule, but the model broke the rule and stayed in German.

Verdict of the first study. The pre-registered test did not run, so free-generation steering is not established by our own standard. Exploratory analysis says that the word-order direction changes the order in which the model writes verbs and noun objects in Russian and German. The French effect is an artefact of object type, and the Hindi effect rests on few pairs. The change occurs only at a strength that also degrades the text. A push against a fixed order moves the model out of the language instead of reordering it.

A confirmatory test (pre-registered)

The second study tests the exploratory findings on fresh prompts and on four new languages. The exploratory analysis made three choices after the fact: inclusion per direction, the strength k = 2, and noun objects as the measure. The second study fixes these choices in advance. We committed the pre-registration and the frozen code before any generation.

  • Prompts. 150 FLORES+ devtest sentences that the first study did not use, in 9 languages, each cut after the first 40% of its words: 1,350 prompts. We took them from the same seeded shuffle, continued past its first 150 sentences.
  • Languages with flexible order. In these languages the grammar lets a noun object stand on either side of its verb. German and Dutch put the verb last in subordinate and non-finite clauses, and second in main clauses. Russian, Ukrainian, Polish and Croatian have verb-object basic order with free reordering.
  • Controls with rigid order: English and Spanish (noun objects after the verb) and Korean (verb-final).
  • Directions. Layer 14, estimated without the test language, by the procedure of the preference test. The procedure gives exactly the same German, English and Russian directions as before. For each language, 24 random directions at the same norm.
  • Conditions. Fixed strength k = ±2, without calibration. Unsteered; word-order direction at ±2; Indo-European direction at ±2; 24 random directions at ±2. These 53 conditions give 71,550 continuations.
  • Generation and measurement. Greedy decoding of 40 new tokens, cut at the first newline. The language identifier is restricted to the 9 languages, and Stanza uses the default model of each language. The counting rule is that of the first study. The primary measure is the share of noun objects (NOUN or PROPN) before their verb.

Before any generation, the held-out projections place the new languages as expected: Dutch −0.08, Ukrainian −0.13, Polish −0.21, Croatian −0.16, Spanish −0.35 and Korean +0.52. With n^{\text{N}}_{\text{OV}}(x) and n^{\text{N}}_{\text{VO}}(x) the counted noun-object pairs of a continuation in each order, the primary measure and the slope are

\rho^{\text{N}}_i(kv)=\frac{\sum_{x\in X_i(kv)}n^{\text{N}}_{\text{OV}}(x)}{\sum_{x\in X_i(kv)}\big(n^{\text{N}}_{\text{OV}}(x)+n^{\text{N}}_{\text{VO}}(x)\big)},\qquad b_i(v)=\frac{\rho^{\text{N}}_i(+2v)-\rho^{\text{N}}_i(-2v)}{4}.

The slope b_i(v) is defined only if the unsteered condition, +2v and -2v each have at least 20 noun-object pairs. For a set of languages H, the pooled slope \bar b_H(v) is the mean of the defined b_i(v), i\in H. The null distribution is the pooled slopes of the 24 random directions over the same set, each with its own inclusion. A random direction with no defined language drops out. Then z_H=\big(\bar b_H(\beta_{\text{OV}})-\overline{\bar b_H(r)}\big)/\mathrm{sd}\big(\bar b_H(r)\big).

Hypotheses and claim rule (pre-registered)

H1, replication on fresh prompts: H=\{\text{German},\text{Russian}\}. Evaluable if the word-order direction has a defined slope in both languages.

H2, generalization to new languages: H=\{\text{Dutch},\text{Ukrainian},\text{Polish},\text{Croatian}\}. Evaluable if the word-order direction has a defined slope in at least 3 of the 4 languages.

Each hypothesis also needs a defined pooled slope for at least 20 of the 24 random directions. Claim: the hypothesis is evaluable, \bar b_H(\beta_{\text{OV}}) exceeds every defined random pooled slope, and z_H>2.58 (one-sided 0.005, a Bonferroni correction of 0.01 over the two hypotheses). If a hypothesis is not evaluable, we make no claim and report it as not evaluable.

The secondary predictions carry no claim. Under k = +2, the noun-object share rises in the four Slavic languages. Under k = −2, it falls in German and Dutch. In English, Spanish and Korean, the word-order slope stays inside the central 95% of that language's random slopes. We also report the Indo-European slope and the shares for pronoun objects and for all objects. Further secondary results are the rate of continuations in the prompt language, the sign-specific language loss and the repetition.

The expected effect comes from the first study, where the noun-object slope was +18.1 points per unit of k in German and +7.7 in Russian. We ran a synthetic check with the analysis code, with the exploratory effect sizes for H1 and half of them for H2. Both hypotheses were claimed in 20 of 20 simulations. When the word-order direction behaved like a random direction, the claim rate was 0 of 100 for H1 and 1 of 100 for H2. The pre-registration fixes the reading of the outcomes:

  • H1 and H2 hold: the direction controls the order of noun objects in languages whose grammar allows the choice, at a strength that also costs fluency.
  • H1 holds and H2 fails: the effect is specific to German and Russian, not a general property of languages with flexible order.
  • H1 fails: the exploratory result does not replicate on fresh prompts.

Results of the confirmatory test

This subsection reports the pre-registered result of the second study. The replication (H1) holds. The generalization (H2) could not be evaluated, because two of the four new languages left their language under the push. The run produced the planned 71,550 continuations.

The replication holds. On fresh prompts, the word-order direction changes the order of noun objects in German and Russian. The pooled slope is +15.0 percentage points per unit of k, above all 21 defined random slopes (z = 7.83). The test on four new languages is not evaluable, so it makes no claim. In the two new languages that could be measured, Dutch and Ukrainian, the change is as large (descriptive).

H1, replication (pre-registered: the claim holds). German and Russian both have a defined slope. In German, the share of noun objects before their verb falls from 66% unsteered (68 pairs) to 8% at k = −2 (38 pairs). At k = +2 it is 73%.

In Russian, the share rises from 4% (78 pairs) to 58% at k = +2 (36 pairs); at k = −2 it is 3%. The pooled slope is +15.0 points per unit of k. On average, the share therefore differs by 60 points between k = −2 and k = +2.

Of the 24 random directions, 21 have a defined pooled slope over German and Russian. They give +0.2 ± 1.9 points, with a largest value of +4.2. The word-order slope exceeds all of them, with z = 7.83. The empirical p-value is 1/22 = 0.045, the smallest possible value with 21 random directions. Per language, z is 4.97 in German and 16.7 in Russian. The Indo-European control gives −0.8 points.

A prompt bootstrap, which we did not pre-register, puts the 95% interval of the pooled slope at +11.8 to +18.2 points. The German change is similar to that of the first study (72% to 14% there). The Russian change is larger (1% to 36% there).

H2, generalization (pre-registered: not evaluable). Only Dutch and Ukrainian have a defined slope. The rule required 3 of the 4 languages, so H2 makes no claim. Polish and Croatian drop out at k = +2, the push toward object-first, which is against their majority order. Only 27% of Polish and 37% of Croatian continuations stay in the prompt language. This leaves 2 and 17 noun-object pairs, against the minimum of 20.

Most of the other Polish continuations are English, Croatian or German, and most of the other Croatian continuations are English or German. The steered text in both languages also repeats more. The share of repeated word bigrams under the word-order direction is 14.4% in Polish and 16.0% in Croatian, against 3.8% and 5.3% unsteered. This is the failure that the first study showed: pushed against its order, the model leaves the language instead of reordering it.

Descriptively, the two measurable languages move as strongly as German and Russian. In Dutch, the noun-object share falls from 70% (91 pairs) to 10% at k = −2 (51 pairs); per-language z = 4.94. In Ukrainian, it rises from 3% (62 pairs) to 54% at k = +2 (26 pairs); z = 12.3. Pooled over these two languages, the slope is +15.0 points per unit of k (prompt bootstrap +8.3 to +18.7). The largest random pooled slope is +4.0. These numbers describe the data; they are not a pre-registered test.

Controls and secondary results (pre-registered, no claim).

  • Rigid controls. In English and Spanish, the noun-object share stays between 0% and 1% in all three conditions. As predicted, the slope is inside the central 95% of the random slopes. In Korean, the share is 100% unsteered and at k = +2. At k = −2, only 71% of continuations stay Korean and no noun-object pair is counted, so the Korean prediction cannot be checked.
  • Directional predictions. All four that can be checked hold. The share falls under k = −2 in German and Dutch, and rises under k = +2 in Russian and Ukrainian.
  • Indo-European control. −0.8 points per unit of k over the H1 languages and +0.9 over the H2 languages.
  • Sign-specific language loss. Continuations stay in the prompt language in 76% of cases when the push is against the language's order. They stay in 95% of cases when it is toward it. The rate is lower against in 7 of 9 languages, but the sign test gives p = 0.18. The difference that the first study found (p = 0.016, exploratory) is therefore not confirmed at the 0.05 level.
  • Random directions. In every language, between 6 and 18 of the 48 random conditions have fewer than 20 noun-object pairs. A rule that needs every condition, as in the first study, would therefore again have excluded every language. Inclusion per direction kept H1 evaluable.

The pre-registered readings did not include this outcome: H1 holds, and H2 could not be tested.

Verdict of the confirmatory test. The word-order direction changes the order in which Qwen2.5-7B writes noun objects in German and Russian, on prompts that it had not seen. The exploratory finding is confirmed. Generalization to other languages with flexible order is not established. Dutch and Ukrainian show the same large change, but only descriptively, and Polish and Croatian left their language when pushed against their order. All effects need the strength k = 2, at which some random directions also push a language out of itself.

A pre-registered replication of H1 in Llama-3.1-8B did not pass (pooled slope +4.4 points per unit of k, z = 1.86). It is reported with the within-language direction, in Steering in a second model.
