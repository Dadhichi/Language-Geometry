Figures 5 and 6 measure a preference between two strings that we wrote; the model never has to produce either. The stronger question is whether the same push changes the order in which the model itself places verbs and objects when it writes freely. That is harder: the model has to commit to an object before its verb, and every language has its own rules for when that is allowed.

Setup. We took 150 FLORES+ sentences in eight languages and cut each after the first 40% of its characters, giving 1,200 prompts with the same content in every language. The model continues each prompt greedily for 40 tokens. We steer exactly as before, h^{14}_t\leftarrow h^{14}_t+k\,v at every position, where v is the held-out word-order direction of the prompt's language, the Indo-European direction rescaled to the same norm, or one of 24 random directions of that norm in the span of the language offsets. Fifty-five conditions (unsteered; word order at ±1 and ±2; Indo-European at ±2; 24 random directions at ±2) give 66,000 continuations.

Measurement

Each prompt and its continuation are parsed with Stanza's Universal Dependencies model for the prompt language [12]. For a continuation x, let A(x) be the set of verb–object dependencies (v,o) in which o attaches to v with relation \texttt{obj}, v is tagged VERB, and both words lie inside the generated text. Then

n_{\text{OV}}(x)=\big|\{(v,o)\in A(x):\ \mathrm{pos}(o)<\mathrm{pos}(v)\}\big|,\qquad \rho_\ell(c)=\frac{\sum_{x\in X_\ell(c)}n_{\text{OV}}(x)}{\sum_{x\in X_\ell(c)}\lvert A(x)\rvert},

where X_\ell(c) holds the continuations of language \ell's prompts under condition c that a language identifier (langid, restricted to the eight languages) assigns to \ell. Counting only these keeps a switch into another language from being scored as a change of word order.

The effect of a direction v on language \ell is the slope of the object-first share in the steering strength,

b_\ell(v)=\frac{\rho_\ell(+k^\star v)-\rho_\ell(-k^\star v)}{2k^\star},

a change in share per unit of k. The strength k^\star was fixed in advance by a rule that looks only at random directions: the largest k\in\{0.5,1,2\} at which three random directions keep at least 80% of the unsteered rate of continuations in the prompt language, measured on 20 calibration prompts per language. The rule chose k^\star=2 (88.8% of continuations in the prompt language, against 98.8% unsteered). The pre-registered claim was that the average of b_\ell(\beta_{\text{OV}}) over the included languages exceeds the averages of all 24 random directions, with z>2.33. A language was to be included if it had at least 20 counted dependencies in every one of the 53 conditions the test needs.

The pre-registered test could not be evaluated

No language met the inclusion rule. Random directions of norm 2\lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert are mostly harmless, but a few of them push a given language almost entirely out of itself. Of the 48 random conditions, between 3 and 9 per language left fewer than half of the continuations in the prompt language. Chinese was the only language no random condition did this to, and it lost its own language under the word-order direction instead. Each such condition leaves too few dependencies to count, and because the rule required all 53 conditions to be usable, it excluded every language. The calibration had seen 3 of the 24 directions on 20 prompts, too few to reveal this tail.

The design was inconsistent: the strength rule bounded the average damage done by random directions, while the inclusion rule needed every direction to be harmless. By the rule we committed to, this experiment makes no claim in either direction. Everything below was decided after seeing the data and should be read as description.

What the generations show (exploratory)

The natural repair is to decide inclusion separately for each direction. Language \ell enters the average for v if the unsteered condition and both \pm k^\star v have at least 20 counted dependencies:

\bar b(v)=\frac1{\lvert\mathcal L_v\rvert}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal L_v}b_\ell(v),\qquad \mathcal L_v=\Big\{\ell:\ \min_{c\in\{0,\,+k^\star v,\,-k^\star v\}}\ \sum_{x\in X_\ell(c)}\lvert A(x)\rvert\ \ge 20\Big\}.

For the word-order direction, \mathcal L_v contains five languages: English, French, German, Hindi and Russian. Japanese, Turkish and Chinese drop out because the word-order direction itself, pushed against their order, makes the model leave the language. Figure 7 shows the rates behind the slopes.

The pattern mirrors Figure 6. Each language moves only toward the order it does not already have. Verb-first Russian becomes more object-first under +k and is unchanged under -k (French appears to do the same, but this turns out to be an artefact, explained below), while object-first-leaning German and Hindi become more verb-first under -k and are unchanged under +k. Russian is the cleanest case: its grammar allows the object before the verb for emphasis or style, the steered continuations remain fluent Russian, and the object-first share rises from 9% to 51%, against a random-direction range of 2–18%. German shows a different side of the same push. Steered toward verb-first, the model writes German with English verb placement, which German grammar does not allow in these clauses. In the examples below, verbs are in bold and objects underlined.

(… with the acid in the toothpaste, and forms an alkali.) k = +2 … и кожу разъест. (… and will corrode the skin; literally "and the skin will-corrode".)

Averaged over the five usable languages, the word-order direction changes the object-first share by \bar b(\beta_{\text{OV}})={+6.8} percentage points per unit of k, or 27 points between k={-2} and k={+2}. The 24 random directions give +0.1\pm0.7 (largest +1.1), and the Indo-European direction +0.8 (Figure 8). The word-order slope exceeds every random slope (z=10.0). It also does so when each random direction is compared on exactly the languages both can use (24 of 24), and a bootstrap over prompts puts it between +4.3 and +8.6. Per language, it exceeds all random slopes in Russian (z=5.9), French (4.1), Hindi (2.2) and German (1.9), and is zero in English. As the next part shows, the French number does not reflect reordering.

How much of this is reordering?

The object-first share is a ratio, so it can move without any object moving: it rises if continuations simply contain fewer verb-first objects. Three checks bound the reading.

Object type. Romance languages place unstressed object pronouns before the verb as a rule (French je le vois, "I see it"), and Russian often does. A push that produced more pronouns, or fewer nouns, would raise the share without reordering anything. We re-parsed the five usable languages under the unsteered condition, the word-order direction and all 48 random conditions, and split each verb–object pair by the object's part of speech. The re-parse reproduces the original counts exactly for 94–97% of continuations, depending on the language.

German and Russian reorder nouns. In German, the share of noun objects before their verb falls from 72% to 14% under k={-2}, while pronouns stay in front (96% and 92%). In Russian, it rises from 1% to 36% under k={+2}. French does not reorder at all: not one of its 218 noun objects precedes its verb in any of the three conditions. Its share rises under k={+2} because noun objects all but disappear (6 left, from 100), leaving mostly preverbal clitics. In Hindi, the share of noun objects before the verb falls from 92% to 53%, but on only 17 pairs. The rest of its drop comes from objects tagged as neither noun nor pronoun (19 of 20 after the verb), which do not occur unsteered at all. Counting noun objects alone, with the same per-direction inclusion (English, German and Russian qualify), the slope is +8.6 points per unit of k against a random largest of +2.8 (z=8.2).

Text quality. With w_1\dots w_n the words of a continuation, its repetition is 1-\big\lvert\{(w_i,w_{i+1})\}_{i<n}\big\rvert/(n-1), the share of word bigrams that are repeats. Averaged over the five languages, it is 4.6% unsteered, 7.5% under the word-order direction, 6.8% under the Indo-European direction and 6.1% under random directions (single random conditions range from 0% to 25%). The word-order push degrades text somewhat more than a typical random push of the same size, and far less than the worst.

Strength. At k={\pm1} the slope is near zero in every language except Hindi (+15.7 points per unit of k; at most +1.5 elsewhere). The change appears only at the strength where random directions begin to break language identity, so the push that reorders is also a push that damages.

Language identity is part of the axis

When the word-order direction makes the model leave a language, it does so on one side only. For each language, call the sign of k that pushes it away from its own majority order against, and the other sign toward. Continuations stay in the prompt language 64% of the time when pushed against, and 96% when pushed toward. Against is lower in all seven languages where the two differ (two-sided sign test, p = 0.016); in Hindi they tie at 99%. Japanese pushed toward verb-first mostly turns into Chinese, and French pushed toward object-first mostly into Japanese. But Chinese pushed toward object-first turns into English, so the destination is not simply a language with the target order.

The direction was estimated as a difference between groups of languages, so it is no surprise that it carries some of what distinguishes those languages as languages. Behaviourally, in Japanese, Turkish and Chinese, whose order is fixed, the model more often abandons the language than breaks its order. German, whose verb placement is also fixed by rule, is the exception: there the model broke the rule and stayed in German.

Verdict. The pre-registered test did not run, so free-generation steering is not established by our own standard. Exploratory analysis says the word-order direction does change the order in which the model writes verbs and objects. The change is clear for noun objects in Russian and German; the French effect is an artefact of object type, and the Hindi effect rests on few pairs. But it happens only at a strength that also degrades text, and pushed against a fixed order it moves the model out of the language rather than reordering. A confirmatory rerun would decide inclusion per direction, calibrate strength on the worst random direction rather than the average, and report object type as part of the primary measure.
