A word-order direction from inside each language

This section estimates a word-order direction from pairs of sentences that differ only in word order, inside each language. It shows three pre-registered results. The within-language direction is aligned with the between-language word-order direction in the middle layers. Steering along it reorders noun objects in German and Dutch. Unlike the between-language direction, it keeps the model in the prompt language.

Why a second direction

The word-order direction \beta_{\text{OV}} is a difference between groups of languages. It therefore can carry part of what makes those languages different as languages. The free-generation studies show this. Pushed against their own order, Japanese, Turkish and Chinese (first study) and Polish and Croatian (confirmatory study) often left their language.

A direction estimated from pairs of sentences in the same language cannot carry language identity, because both sentences of a pair are in one language. We call it the within-language direction d_W. The study asks three questions, each pre-registered with a claim (commit 14fa22e):

  1. A, alignment. Does d_W point along \beta_{\text{OV}}?
  2. B, reordering. Does steering along d_W change the order of noun objects?
  3. C, retention. Does d_W keep the model in the prompt language better than \beta_{\text{OV}} does?

The within-language direction

We use the 999 minimal pairs of the preference test, in 8 languages. Each pair holds an attested sentence and its twin with the verb and its object swapped, with the same tokens. For each sentence we take the mean-pooled residual stream over the content tokens, at every layer. This is the same pooling as for the language centroids. For pair p, let

\Delta_p=\bar h\big(x^{\text{OV}}_p\big)-\bar h\big(x^{\text{VO}}_p\big).

The difference mixes two effects: the change of order, and the change from an attested sentence to a constructed one, which is less natural. If the attested order is VO, the OV twin is the constructed one; if it is OV, the VO twin is. We separate the two effects by weighted least squares, per layer and per coordinate:

\Delta_p=d_W+s_p\,d_U+e_p,\qquad s_p=\begin{cases}+1&\text{attested order VO}\\-1&\text{attested order OV}\end{cases},\qquad w_p=\frac{1}{n_{\ell(p)}} .

Here d_U takes the unnaturalness of the constructed twin, and d_W is the within-language word-order direction. The weight w_p divides by the number of pairs of the pair's language \ell(p), so that every language counts equally.

  • Validation. With planted order and unnaturalness vectors and the real pair counts, the estimator recovers both (cosine 0.998 and 1.000). A plain mean of the differences does not (cosine 0.92 with the order vector, 0.46 with the unnaturalness vector). At layer 14 the two estimated components have a cosine of 0.54, so a plain mean would mix them.
  • Held-out versions. For claim A we fit d_W on all 8 pair languages. For steering German, Russian and English, which are pair languages, we fit it without that language's pairs.
  • Reliability. The cosine between estimates from two halves of the pairs is 0.85 to 0.94 at layers 2 to 28, and 0.54 at layer 1.
  • Layer 0. The two twins contain the same tokens, so their mean-pooled embeddings are identical and d_W is zero up to rounding. Layer 0 carries no information in this study.
  • Norm. At layer 14 the natural norm of d_W is 1.24. For steering, we rescale it to \lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert of the prompt language, about 9.4 times larger, so that both directions have the same norm.

A. Alignment (pre-registered: holds)

At layer 14 we compare d_W with \beta_{\text{OV}} estimated from all 34 languages. The null distribution uses 10,000 random unit vectors, uniform in the 33-dimensional span of the 34 centred dev centroids. This is the space in which \beta_{\text{OV}} lies. The claim needs a positive cosine above the 99.9th percentile of the null.

The cosine is +0.232, against a 99.9th percentile of +0.206 (null standard deviation 0.071; p = 0.0002). The claim holds. Most of d_W lies outside the span of language differences: the span holds 16% of its squared norm. Inside the span, the cosine with \beta_{\text{OV}} is +0.58, and

\cos(d_W,\beta_{\text{OV}})=\sqrt{\tfrac{\lVert P d_W\rVert^2}{\lVert d_W\rVert^2}}\;\cos(P d_W,\beta_{\text{OV}})\approx\sqrt{0.16}\times0.58\approx0.23,

where P projects onto the span. The unnaturalness component is not aligned: \cos(d_U,\beta_{\text{OV}})={+0.003}.

The Indo-European control is not near zero. The pre-registration expected \cos(d_W,\beta_{\text{IE}}) near 0. It is −0.18 at layer 14, about 2.5 null standard deviations below zero, and between −0.15 and −0.24 at layers 6 to 18. This expectation therefore did not hold.

We give one possible reason, which we did not test. In our sample, OV order and Indo-European membership are negatively associated: 4 of 16 Indo-European languages are OV, against 7 of 18 others. In the held-out fits used for steering, \beta_{\text{OV}} and \beta_{\text{IE}} themselves are almost orthogonal (cosine −0.03 to +0.02).

B. Reordering (pre-registered: holds)

We repeat the confirmatory free-generation run with d_W: the same 1,350 prompts in 9 languages, greedy decoding, steering at block 14 at every position. The conditions are unsteered; d_W at k\in\{\pm1,\pm2,\pm3\}; and \beta_{\text{OV}} at k=\pm2, run again for a paired comparison. The measure and the inclusion rule are those of the confirmatory study: noun-object share, and at least 20 noun-object pairs unsteered, at +2 and at −2.

  • Statistic. The pooled slope \bar b(d_W): the mean of b_i(d_W)=\big(\rho^{\text{N}}_i(+2d_W)-\rho^{\text{N}}_i(-2d_W)\big)/4 over the included languages of the flexible set (German, Dutch, Russian, Ukrainian, Polish, Croatian).
  • Null. The 24 random directions of the confirmatory study, pooled over the same set, each with its own inclusion.
  • Claim. At least 3 languages included for d_W, at least 20 random directions defined, \bar b(d_W) above every random pooled slope, and z>2.58.

The unsteered and \beta_{\text{OV}} continuations are 100% identical to those of the confirmatory study, because greedy decoding is deterministic here. The random null from that study is therefore exact for this run.

All 6 flexible languages are included. The pooled slope is +3.5 percentage points per unit of k. The 24 random directions give −0.1 ± 0.8, with a largest value of +1.7. The slope exceeds all of them, with z = 4.56 (empirical p = 1/25 = 0.04). The claim holds.

Two languages carry the effect: German (+10.3 points per unit of k) and Dutch (+8.5). The Slavic slopes are near zero: Croatian +1.5, Ukrainian +0.6, Russian +0.1 and Polish 0.0. In German and Dutch the change goes both ways:

At k=\pm1 the shares stay near the unsteered value (German 71% and 70%, Dutch 72% and 73%). The pooled slope is about zero at k = 1 (−0.05 points per unit of k), +3.5 at k = 2 and +5.1 at k = 3. In the controls, the share stays at 0% to 2% in English and Spanish and at 100% in Korean, at every strength of d_W.

C. Language retention (pre-registered: holds)

For each of the 9 languages, the against sign of k pushes the language away from its own majority order. At the against sign and k = 2, we compare the share of continuations in the prompt language under d_W and under \beta_{\text{OV}}, in the same run. The claim needs claim B and a higher share for d_W in at least 8 of the 9 languages.

Under d_W, 98% of continuations stay in the prompt language on average, against 76% under \beta_{\text{OV}}. The share is higher for d_W in 8 of the 9 languages, and Dutch ties (99% under both). The sign test over the 8 languages that differ gives p = 0.008. The claim holds. The largest differences are in Polish (98% against 27%), Croatian (95% against 37%) and Korean (100% against 71%).

The text also degrades less. At k = +2, the share of repeated word bigrams is 5.2% in Polish and 9.3% in Croatian under d_W. Under \beta_{\text{OV}} it is 24.8% and 26.0%, and unsteered it is 3.8% and 5.3%.

All three pre-registered claims hold. The word-order direction that the model uses inside each language lies along the between-language word-order axis in the middle layers (cosine +0.23, p = 0.0002). Steering along it reorders noun objects (pooled over six flexible-order languages, z = 4.56; the effect comes from German and Dutch) and keeps the model in the prompt language (98% against 76%).

How much of the between-language effect runs through the within-language direction? (exploratory)

The following argument uses arithmetic that we did after seeing the data. Both directions are scaled to \lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert. A push of k\,\beta_{\text{OV}} therefore contains a component along d_W equal to a push along d_W of strength

k_{\text{eff}}=k\,\cos(\beta_{\text{OV}},d_W),\qquad \text{component}=k\,\cos(\beta_{\text{OV}},d_W)\,\lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert .

Per flexible language, the cosine of the steering d_W with \beta_{\text{OV}} is 0.20 to 0.23, so at k = 2 we get k_{\text{eff}} = 0.40 to 0.47. Even at the larger strength k = ±1, d_W has no clear effect. For example, German is at 71%, 66% and 70% at k = −1, 0 and +1, and Russian at 7%, 4% and 5%. Yet \beta_{\text{OV}} at k = −2 moves German to 8%, and at k = +2 Russian to 58%.

Assume that the effects of the components add without interaction; we did not test this. Then most of the effect of \beta_{\text{OV}} on word order runs through components orthogonal to d_W. This agrees with two exploratory observations. \beta_{\text{OV}} makes the model write German with English verb placement, and it moves Polish and Croatian out of the language. In contrast, d_W reorders inside the language.

What this means, and its limits

The between-language word-order axis contains the model's own within-language word-order feature, in the middle layers. Steering along that feature reorders noun objects in German and Dutch without a change of language. By the exploratory argument above, the extra Slavic reordering of \beta_{\text{OV}} and its language switching probably come from components that d_W does not share. Several limits apply:

  • All states are mean-pooled over the sentence, so d_W is a sentence-level summary, not a per-token feature.
  • For steering, d_W is about 9.4 times its natural norm at layer 14. The strengths that reorder are large for this direction.
  • The pairs come from 8 languages. Of the 6 flexible-order generation languages, only German and Russian have pairs.
  • One model (Qwen2.5-7B), one steering layer (14), and an alignment cosine of 0.23: most of d_W lies outside the span of language differences.
  • The Indo-European control did not behave as expected, and we do not know why.
