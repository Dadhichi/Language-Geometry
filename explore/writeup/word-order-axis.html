The Word-Order Axis

Two multilingual language models arrange 34 languages by descent and by word order. The two structures occupy different depths. Steering along the word-order structure changes which order the model prefers, and in German and Russian the order in which it writes.

Summary

We study how two multilingual language models, Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B, place languages relative to one another in their residual stream. Each model reads the same 2,009 sentences in 34 languages (FLORES+ translations). We represent each language by the mean of its sentence representations at each layer, and we analyse the distances between these 34 means.

Linguists classify languages in two independent ways. One is descent, in family trees such as Indo-European. The other is type, with properties such as the order of verb and object. We ask whether either classification appears in the distances after we remove the effects of script, shared tokens and sentence length. Then we ask whether the model uses what we find. The main results are:

  • Two structures, each beyond the other. The distances contain the Glottolog family tree and a split between the 11 object-before-verb languages and the rest. Each structure remains significant when the other is held fixed, in two geometries and in both models (all p ≤ 0.0007; Bonferroni threshold 0.0125). Pre-registered; replicated in the second model.
  • Different depths. The word-order signal is near zero at the embedding, has its maximum between 25% and 71% of the depth, and falls in the last layers. The family-tree signal is strongest in the first and last layers, where the model reads and writes tokens. Descriptive.
  • The axis is head direction. Object–verb order and adposition order each explain the distances beyond the other in 3 of 4 analyses. The axis is therefore a bundle of head-final features. Object–verb order has the largest unique share in 3 of 4. The family tree is not explained by vocabulary that the languages share. Pre-registered.
  • The model uses the axis. We add a direction, estimated from 33 other languages, to layer 14 of Qwen2.5-7B. It shifts the model's preference between the object-before-verb and verb-before-object versions of 999 real sentences. The shift occurs in all 8 test languages, with z = 4.99 against 32 random directions of the same norm. Pre-registered.
  • The axis also changes what the model writes, in German and Russian. Our first pre-registered test of free generation could not be evaluated, because its inclusion rule excluded all 8 languages. Exploratory analysis then found that the direction reorders noun objects in Russian and German. A second pre-registered test on fresh prompts confirmed it. The share of noun objects before the verb moves by +15.0 percentage points per unit of strength (z = 7.83). It falls from 66% to 8% in German at k = −2 and rises from 4% to 58% in Russian at k = +2. Pre-registered replication: confirmed.
  • Generalization to other languages is open. The same test on four new languages with flexible order was not evaluable. Dutch and Ukrainian changed as much as German and Russian, but Polish and Croatian, pushed against their order, left their language. The effect needs a strength at which the push also degrades text. Pre-registered: not evaluable; Dutch and Ukrainian descriptive.
  • Inside each language, the model has a word-order feature that lies along the axis. We estimated a direction from sentence pairs that differ only in word order. In the middle layers it points along the between-language word-order direction (cosine +0.23 at layer 14, p = 0.0002). Steering along it reorders noun objects in German and Dutch (z = 4.56). It keeps 98% of continuations in the prompt language, against 76% for the between-language direction. Pre-registered; all three claims hold. The Indo-European control was not near zero, as we had expected.
  • Token by token, language identity switches fast. The representation encodes graded uncertainty about the language when the evidence is ambiguous. At a code switch, it moves to the new language within about one token, faster than a Bayesian observer of the whole sequence. Pre-registered; graded belief supported, Bayesian switching not supported.
Evidence labels

Pre-registered: we committed the hypothesis, the test, the threshold and the analysis code to the repository before we extracted or analysed the data. The commit of each pre-registration is in the methods.

Exploratory: we decided the analysis after we saw the data. These results describe the data. They are not tests of a prior hypothesis.

Descriptive: a pre-registered quantity that we report without a claim, for example a per-layer profile.

Languages as points

This section states the question, the prior work, and the terms that we use in the rest of the article.

Interpretability research often finds that a model represents a familiar object with a shape that matches the structure of the object. Days of the week and months lie on circles. Numbers lie on helices. When a model counts the characters on a line, the count lies on a curved manifold. Attention heads rotate this manifold to compare the count with the line width [1].

Languages also have structure. Linguists classify them in two independent ways. The first is descent: languages with a common ancestor form a family, and each family forms a tree. For example, Indo-European has Germanic, Romance and Slavic branches. The second is type: languages differ in properties such as the order of the verb and its object, independently of their ancestry.

English (VO) The dog chased the ball. (verb before object)
Turkish (OV) Köpek topu kovaladı. (dog ball chased: object before verb)

A model that learns from text in many languages never sees a label for either property. We ask four questions about the arrangement of languages inside the model:

  1. Does the arrangement contain the family tree?
  2. Does it contain a word-order structure, separately from the family tree?
  3. At which depth does each structure occur?
  4. Does the model use the word-order structure when it processes text?

Earlier work shows that multilingual representations have a language-neutral part and a language-specific part. Subtracting the mean representation of each language improves cross-lingual retrieval [2][3]. Language means cluster loosely by family [3], and family trees can be recovered from the output distributions of models [8]. Decoder models process many languages through shared middle-layer features [4][7]. We know of no earlier test that separates descent from type and from surface overlap, such as shared script and shared tokens. This article gives such a test for decoder models, across depth, with a causal check.

Terms used in this article

Layer ℓ: the residual stream after block ℓ; layer 0 is the output of the embedding. Depth: ℓ divided by the number of blocks.

Centroid and offset: the mean representation of a language, and the centroid minus the mean of all 34 centroids.

Geometry: the inner product with which we measure distances between offsets (causal or whitened, defined in Setup).

Split: a division of the 34 languages into two groups. Family tree: the 20 splits of the Glottolog tree. Word-order split: the 11 object-before-verb (OV) languages against the 23 others.

Gain: the fraction of the remaining squared-distance error that a set of splits removes (defined in Testing for a tree and an axis).

Axis: the word-order structure in the arrangement of the languages. Direction: a vector in the residual stream that we add to the model to steer it. Strength k: the multiple of a direction that we add.

Setup

This section defines the models, the languages, the sentence representations, the two geometries, and an unbiased estimate of the squared distance between two languages.

Models. Qwen2.5-7B has 28 transformer blocks and a residual width of 3,584. Llama-3.1-8B has 32 blocks and a width of 4,096. We use the pretrained base checkpoints, not the instruction-tuned ones. Both run in bfloat16 without quantization.

Text. FLORES+ [9] gives the same sentences, professionally translated, in more than 200 languages. It has two disjoint halves: dev (997 sentences) and devtest (1,012 sentences).

Languages. We chose 34 languages to separate family, script and word order. They cover 10 families and 12 scripts. The 11 object-before-verb languages come from 5 families. Three pairs are one language in two scripts: Hindi and Urdu, Croatian and Serbian, and Chinese in simplified and traditional characters.

Sentence representations

Let h^\ell_t(x)\in\mathbb R^d be the residual stream at position t and layer \ell for an input x=(x_0,x_1,\dots,x_T). Every input starts with the same sink token x_0: the BOS token for Llama, and a newline for Qwen, which has no BOS token. In the middle layers the residual stream at position 0 has a norm much larger than at other positions. We therefore drop position 0 and average over the content positions:

\bar h^\ell(x)=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}h^\ell_t(x).

Let x_{i,s} be sentence s in language i. On one half of FLORES with n sentences, the centroid and the offset of language i are

\mu_i^\ell=\frac1n\sum_{s=1}^{n}\bar h^\ell(x_{i,s}),\qquad a_i^\ell=\mu_i^\ell-\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu_j^\ell,\qquad N=34.

All results in this article concern the 34 offsets and the distances between them. We write f_i for the mean number of content tokens per sentence of language i (its fertility).

Two geometries

A squared distance \lVert a\rVert_M^2=a^\top Ma needs a positive semi-definite matrix M. We use two, and every pre-registered claim must hold in at least one of them without a reversal in the other.

M_{\text{causal}}=\operatorname{diag}(\omega)\,\operatorname{Cov}_{y}\!\big[\gamma(y)\big]\,\operatorname{diag}(\omega),\qquad M_{\text{wh}}=\Big((1-\eta)\,W+\eta\,\tfrac{\operatorname{tr}W}{d}\,I\Big)^{-1},\quad \eta=\tfrac12 .
  • Causal geometry. \gamma(y) is the unembedding row of token y, and \omega is the weight vector of the final RMSNorm. The covariance runs over the real vocabulary: 151,665 tokens for Qwen, and the 128,000 non-special tokens for Llama. This is the causal inner product of Park et al. [5], under which the length of a direction measures how much it moves the logits. We apply it at every layer as an approximation in the style of the logit lens.
  • Whitened geometry. W is the pooled within-language covariance of the sentence representations on the dev half, W=\frac{1}{N(n-1)}\sum_{i}\sum_{s}\big(\bar h(x_{i,s})-\mu_i\big)\big(\bar h(x_{i,s})-\mu_i\big)^\top. Distances are then in units of the variation between sentences of one language. The shrinkage \eta keeps the inverse stable in 3,584 or 4,096 dimensions.

Unbiased squared distances

A squared distance between estimated offsets is biased upward by the sampling noise of the centroids. With \Sigma_i the covariance of the sentence representations of language i,

\mathbb E\,\lVert\hat a_i-\hat a_j\rVert_M^2\;\approx\;\lVert a_i-a_j\rVert_M^2+\frac{\operatorname{tr}\!\big(M(\Sigma_i+\Sigma_j)\big)}{n}.

Languages whose sentences vary more would therefore look farther apart. To remove this bias, we estimate each offset twice, on dev (\hat a^{(1)}) and on devtest (\hat a^{(2)}), and we cross the two estimates:

K_{ij}=\tfrac12\big(\hat a_i^{(1)\top}M\,\hat a_j^{(2)}+\hat a_j^{(1)\top}M\,\hat a_i^{(2)}\big),\qquad D^2_{ij}=K_{ii}+K_{jj}-2K_{ij}.

The two halves contain different sentences, so their sampling errors are independent and \mathbb E[K_{ij}]=a_i^\top Ma_j. Therefore D^2_{ij} is an unbiased estimate of the squared distance between the true offsets.

We estimate W on the dev half and treat M_{\text{wh}} as fixed in this argument.

The primary statistics use a pre-registered window \mathcal W of middle layers: layers 8–20 of Qwen and layers 9–23 of Llama. Both windows cover the same relative depth, 0.29 to 0.71. Over the window, we average the trace-normalized cross-half Gram matrices:

\bar K=\frac1{|\mathcal W|}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal W}\frac{K^\ell}{\operatorname{tr}K^\ell},\qquad D^2_{ij}=\bar K_{ii}+\bar K_{jj}-2\bar K_{ij}.

Sampling noise in the centroids does not limit the results. In 50 sentence bootstrap resamples, which use the same sentence indices in every language, the 90% intervals of the primary gains have half-widths below 0.001. The number of languages limits the results.

A first look: translations and a trap

This section reports a pilot study that shaped the design of the main study. It shows three things. Languages differ mostly by a constant offset. Mean pooling adds a scale factor per language. Per-language principal component analysis (PCA) creates a false result. All results in this section are exploratory.

The pilot used Qwen2.5-7B and every second layer from 0 to 28. Its 12 languages are English, German, French, Spanish, Russian, Hindi, Arabic, Chinese, Japanese, Turkish, Vietnamese and Indonesian. Four independent analyses used the same activations. We validated every statistic on synthetic data that we sent through the same pipeline.

Languages differ mostly by an offset

To a good approximation, a sentence representation in the middle layers is the sum of four terms:

\bar h^{\ell}(x_{i,s})\;\approx\;\mu_0+a_i+\kappa_i\,\psi_s+\varepsilon_{i,s}.

Here \mu_0 is a common mean and a_i the offset of language i. The vector \psi_s is the content of sentence s that all its translations share. The scalar \kappa_i is a scale factor per language, and \varepsilon_{i,s} is the remainder. The terms differ in size:

The offset alone is enough to match translations. We subtract each language's centroid and match each sentence to the most similar sentence of the other language by cosine similarity:

\hat s=\arg\max_{s'}\ \cos\big(\bar h(x_{i,s})-\mu_i,\ \bar h(x_{j,s'})-\mu_j\big).

At layer 14, this finds the correct translation among the 1,012 devtest sentences in 99.1% of cases, averaged over all ordered pairs of languages. The centroids come from the dev half.

The scale factor comes from mean pooling

The scale factor is a property of the pooling, not of the model. With f_i the mean number of tokens per sentence,

\log\kappa_i\;\approx\;c-\tfrac12\log f_i .

This is the scaling of a mean of weakly correlated vectors. Suppose that the part of each token state that carries sentence content varies from token to token with little correlation. Then the norm of its mean over T tokens falls as T^{-1/2}. Multiplying each sentence vector by \sqrt{T} removes 80% of the variance of \log\kappa_i. Every analysis below therefore includes \lvert\log f_i-\log f_j\rvert as a covariate, and we also report a variant with rescaled pooling.

A trap: maps that seem not to compose

The pilot also gave a false result, and we report it because the mistake is easy to make. We first fitted orthogonal maps between languages, each inside the top-q principal subspace of its own language. Let X_i hold the sentence representations of language i as rows, B_i\in\mathbb R^{d\times q} its top-q principal directions, and Z_i=(X_i-\mu_i)B_i. Then

R_{ij}=\arg\min_{R\in O(q)}\lVert Z_iR-Z_j\rVert_F,\qquad E_{ijm}=\frac{\lVert Z_i\,(R_{ij}R_{jm}-R_{im})\rVert_F^2}{\lVert Z_i\rVert_F^2}.

If one shared representation relates the languages, the maps compose and the composition error E_{ijm} is near 0. Against a null simulation built on this assumption, the real error was 1.5 to 4.5 times too large.

The cause is the truncation to q dimensions. Suppose that the languages are exactly consistent: X_j-\mu_j=(X_i-\mu_i)\,Q_{ij} with orthogonal maps that compose, Q_{ij}Q_{jm}=Q_{im}. The best q\times q map is close to the orthogonal part of T_{ij}=B_i^\top Q_{ij}B_j, and

T_{ij}\,T_{jm}=B_i^\top Q_{ij}\,P_j\,Q_{jm}B_m,\qquad P_j=B_jB_j^\top .

This product equals T_{im}=B_i^\top Q_{im}B_m only if the projector P_j acts as the identity on the directions involved. That is true only if the maps carry the three subspaces exactly onto one another. For subspaces estimated from about 1,000 sentences, this is false: the top-64 subspaces of different languages overlap only partly (mean squared cosine about 0.64). The composition test therefore measured subspace mismatch. Two checks confirm this:

  • Exactly consistent synthetic data, sent through the same pipeline, give E=0.091 at layer 14 with q=64. The real data give 0.077. The original null model predicted 0.017.
  • When all languages use one shared subspace, B_j:=Q_{ij}^\top B_i, the real maps compose as well as consistent synthetic data do (0.0016 against 0.0020).

For this reason, the rest of the article uses distances between centroids, which need no basis per language.

The residual map

This section shows the arrangement of the 34 languages after we remove the effects that we expect from surface properties. The first coordinate of the result separates the languages by word order. The map is descriptive; the next section tests what it shows.

Most of the variation in the squared distances is a star. In a star, the squared distance between two languages is close to the sum of two terms, one for each language. Each term is the distance of that language from the centre, and it depends mostly on the number of tokens per sentence. Languages with long tokenizations, such as Telugu and Khmer, are far from the centre. In Qwen, the star alone explains 52% of the variation of D^2 in the causal geometry and 75% in the whitened geometry. We construct the map as follows:

  1. Compute D^2 from the window mean \bar K in the whitened geometry.
  2. Fit the base terms of the next section (star, shared script, token distance, fertility difference) by non-negative least squares.
  3. Take the residuals r_{ij} of this fit and add a constant so that every off-diagonal residual is non-negative.
  4. Apply classical multidimensional scaling to the residual matrix and keep the first two coordinates.
  5. Orient the first coordinate so that the mean of the object-before-verb languages is positive.

The method receives no information about family or word order. Only step 5 uses word order, and it only fixes the sign of the first coordinate.

Two features of the map guide the tests. First, the word-order groups cut across a family. Hindi, Urdu, Marathi and Persian are Indo-European and object-first; the Germanic, Romance and Slavic languages are not. Second, languages of one family stay close together inside each end. Apart from Indo-Iranian, the object-before-verb group consists of complete families (Turkic, Dravidian, Japanese, Korean). A test must therefore separate the two structures.

Testing for a tree and an axis

This section turns the map into two pre-registered tests. Random evolution along a tree predicts that squared distances are a sum of terms, one for each split of the languages. We use this to measure how much the family tree and the word-order split explain. Both structures pass, each beyond the other, in both geometries and in both models.

From a tree to a regression on splits

Suppose that the offset of each language is a sum of independent random steps along the branches from a root r to that language:

a_i=\sum_{e\,\in\,\mathrm{path}(r,\,i)}\xi_e,\qquad \mathbb E[\xi_e]=0,\quad \mathbb E\big[\xi_e^\top M\xi_e\big]=\sigma_e^2,\quad \xi_e\ \text{independent}.

In the difference of two offsets, the steps on the common part of the two paths cancel. The other steps add in quadrature:

\mathbb E\,\lVert a_i-a_j\rVert_M^2=\sum_{e\,\in\,\mathrm{path}(i,\,j)}\sigma_e^2 .

Each internal branch e divides the languages into those below it and the rest. This is a split S_e. The branch adds \sigma_e^2 to exactly the pairs that its split separates. Each leaf branch adds a term for one language. With the indicator \delta_S(i,j)=\mathbb 1\big[\lvert S\cap\{i,j\}\rvert=1\big], a tree with splits \mathcal S predicts

D^2_{ij}=u_i+u_j+\sum_{S\in\mathcal S}\theta_S\,\delta_S(i,j)+\phi^\top c_{ij}+\epsilon_{ij},\qquad u,\ \theta,\ \phi\ \ge 0 .

The terms u_i form the star. The covariates c_{ij} remove known surface effects. We fit all coefficients by non-negative least squares over the 561 pairs of languages.

Base terms and gain

The base terms are the terms that every test keeps fixed:

  • the star, u_i for each of the 34 languages;
  • one split for each script that more than one language uses: Latin, Cyrillic, Arabic and Devanagari;
  • the token distance 1-\sum_y\min\big(p_i(y),p_j(y)\big), where p_i is the frequency distribution of the model's tokens in the FLORES text of language i;
  • the fertility difference \lvert\log f_i-\log f_j\rvert.

We divide each covariate column by its standard deviation. In Qwen, the base terms explain 85% (causal) and 92% (whitened) of the variation of D^2. We score a set of splits \mathcal S by its gain, the fraction of the remaining squared error that it removes:

G(\mathcal S\mid\mathcal A)=1-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}\big(\mathcal A\cup\mathcal S\big)}{\mathrm{SSE}\big(\mathcal A\big)} .

Here \mathcal A is the base terms plus any splits that we hold fixed, and SSE is the residual sum of squares of the non-negative fit. We write G(\mathcal S) when \mathcal A is the base terms alone.

The two structures and their null distributions

  • Family tree, \mathcal T: the 20 internal splits of the Glottolog tree [11] restricted to our languages. Eleven splits are in Indo-European: Indo-European itself; Germanic; West Germanic (English, German, Dutch); Romance; Ibero-Romance (Spanish, Portuguese); Slavic; East Slavic (Russian, Ukrainian); South Slavic (Croatian, Serbian); Indo-Iranian; Indo-Aryan (Hindi, Urdu, Marathi); Hindi–Urdu. Nine are in other families: Semitic; Arabic–Maltese; Turkic; Oghuz (Turkish, Azerbaijani); Finnic; Chinese; Austronesian; Austroasiatic; Dravidian. The null distribution permutes the leaf labels and keeps the shape of the tree.
  • Word-order split, S_{\text{OV}}: the 11 languages with object-before-verb order in WALS feature 83A [10], against the other 23. WALS does not code Kazakh; we assign it to the OV group because all coded Turkic languages are OV. The null distribution draws random sets of 11 languages.

Each test uses B=10^4 random draws. The p-value is p=\big(1+\#\{b:G_b\ge G_{\text{obs}}\}\big)/(1+B), so the smallest possible value is 0.0001.

Conditional tests and claim rules

The two structures overlap, because the OV group consists mostly of complete subtrees. In simulation, a planted family tree alone makes the marginal word-order test reject, and a planted word-order split alone makes the marginal family-tree test reject. Each claim therefore also needs a conditional test, which holds the other structure fixed: G(\mathcal T\mid S_{\text{OV}}) and G(S_{\text{OV}}\mid\mathcal T). The conditional tests use the same null distributions.

Claim rules (pre-registered)

Family-tree claim: G(\mathcal T) and G(\mathcal T\mid S_{\text{OV}}) both have p < 0.0125 in the same geometry.

Word-order claim: G(S_{\text{OV}}) and G(S_{\text{OV}}\mid\mathcal T) both have p < 0.0125 in the same geometry.

A claim must hold in at least one geometry and must not reverse in the other. The threshold 0.0125 is a Bonferroni correction of 0.05 over {family tree, word order} × {causal, whitened}.

Same-language test (secondary, α = 0.05): Hindi–Urdu, Croatian–Serbian and the two Chinese scripts are closer than predicted. The prediction uses the star, the token distance and the fertility difference, without script terms. The statistic is the mean percentile rank of their three residuals among the 561 pairs. The null distribution draws random sets of three pairs.

The two profiles suggest that the two structures have different sources. The first and last layers read and write tokens. Related languages share word forms, so a family signal at these layers can come from the form of the tokens. But the base terms already remove the unigram token distance. In addition, the character-overlap control of the next section changes the per-layer family-tree gain by at most 0.002 (Qwen) and 0.008 (Llama) at any layer. A source in word form at the edges therefore remains possible, but we do not show it.

Word order is not a property of any single token. It is a property of how tokens are arranged. It appears in the layers that earlier work describes as a shared, largely language-neutral space [4][7]. It also nearly disappears at the last layer, where the model prepares the next token.

Which word-order feature, and is the family tree only shared words?

This section answers two pre-registered questions about the two structures. First, the word-order axis is a bundle of head-final features, not object–verb order alone; object–verb order is its most distinct part. Second, the family tree remains when we control for two measures of shared vocabulary.

A bundle of word-order features

We defined the word-order split by one feature, the order of object and verb. But word-order features occur together. Languages that put the object before the verb usually also put the adposition after its noun: they use postpositions, as in Hindi घर में ("house in"). They also put the possessor before the possessed noun, as in "the house's roof", against French "le toit de la maison". Linguists call this shared property head direction: the head of a phrase comes first or last [10]. The axis can therefore encode object–verb order specifically, or the whole bundle.

Three of our languages break the bundle. Persian puts the object before the verb but uses prepositions. Finnish and Estonian put the verb first but use postpositions. Three languages give little power, but they let us ask whether either feature explains the distances beyond the other, with the family tree held fixed. We define four splits from WALS; for languages that WALS does not code, we use the value of their genus.

We describe the axis as head direction, with object–verb order as its most distinct part. The steering experiments below change the order of verbs and objects, which is the part of the bundle that a minimal edit of a sentence can test.

The family tree is not only shared words

The family tree is strongest where the model handles single tokens, so it can come from shared vocabulary. German Hand and English hand look alike to a tokenizer. The base terms already include the token distance. We added two stronger lexical distances, chosen in advance. The first measures shared character sequences in the FLORES sentences after we write every script in Latin letters. It captures cognates, loanwords and names in the form that the model reads:

\mathrm{LEX}_{ij}=1-\frac1{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}J\Big(G_3\big(\varrho(x_{i,s})\big),\,G_3\big(\varrho(x_{j,s})\big)\Big).

Here \varrho writes a sentence in lower-case Latin letters, G_3 gives its set of character trigrams, and J is the Jaccard index. The mean runs over the dev and devtest sentences. The second distance is the ASJP distance between 40-item basic-vocabulary lists [13]. It is the normalized Levenshtein distance between words for the same concept, divided by the same quantity for different concepts. The division removes chance similarity that comes from similar sound inventories:

\mathrm{LDN}(u,v)=\frac{\mathrm{lev}(u,v)}{\max(\lvert u\rvert,\lvert v\rvert)},\qquad \mathrm{LDND}_{ij}=\frac{\operatorname{mean}_{c}\ \mathrm{LDN}\big(w_{ic},w_{jc}\big)}{\operatorname{mean}_{c\ne c'}\ \mathrm{LDN}\big(w_{ic},w_{jc'}\big)} .

Here w_{ic} is the word of language i for concept c; for synonyms we take the minimum distance. The two lexical distances correlate at 0.66 over the 561 pairs. The ASJP control is too strict by design: linguists use basic vocabulary to define families, so this control removes part of the family tree itself.

  • B1 (claim): G(\mathcal T) with \mathrm{LEX} added to the base terms, against the leaf-permutation null. Claim if p < 0.0125 in both models, in at least one geometry each.
  • B2 (reported): the same with \mathrm{LEX} and \mathrm{LDND} added.

Character overlap explains nothing beyond the token distance. The family-tree gain changes by at most 0.001, for example from 0.294 to 0.293 in Llama's causal geometry, and B1 has p ≤ 0.0011 in all four analyses. Basic vocabulary absorbs between 2% and 56% of the gain, and the rest is still significant in every analysis (p ≤ 0.0002). The claim holds: the family structure in the distances is not a by-product of the words that the languages share in this text.

Is the axis used?

A structure in the representations can describe the data without the model reading it. This section tests use. We add a word-order direction to the residual stream of Qwen2.5-7B and measure a change in behaviour that only word order can explain. The pre-registered test passes: the direction shifts the model's preference between the two orders of the same words, in all 8 test languages.

The word-order direction

At layer 14 we regress the dev-half offsets on language properties. It is one ordinary least-squares regression with all 3,584 coordinates as responses:

a_j=\beta_0+\beta_{\text{OV}}\,\mathbb 1_{\text{OV}}(j)+\beta_{\text{IE}}\,\mathbb 1_{\text{IE}}(j)+\sum_{\sigma\in\{\text{Latin},\,\text{Cyrillic},\,\text{Arabic},\,\text{Devanagari}\}}\beta_\sigma\,\mathbb 1_\sigma(j)+\beta_f\log f_j+e_j .

The coefficient \beta_{\text{OV}}\in\mathbb R^{3584} is a direction in the residual stream. It is the difference between object-before-verb languages and the others, with Indo-European membership, script and fertility held fixed. For each test language i, we fit the regression on the other 33 languages and call the result \beta_{\text{OV}}^{(-i)}. The direction is therefore never fitted on the language that it steers. At layer 14 its norm is between 11.6 and 12.1, and its cosine with \beta_{\text{IE}} is between −0.03 and +0.02.

Before any intervention, we check where each test language falls on its own held-out direction:

\mathrm{proj}_i=\frac{\big\langle a_i,\ \beta^{(-i)}_{\text{OV}}\big\rangle}{\lVert\beta^{(-i)}_{\text{OV}}\rVert^2}.

On this scale, the mean projection of the OV languages is between +0.50 and +0.53. The mean projection of the other languages is between −0.24 and −0.25. The ranges come from the 8 regressions, one for each left-out test language.

Minimal pairs

From Universal Dependencies treebanks [12] in 8 languages, we took 999 real sentences in which a verb and its object are adjacent. For each sentence we built a variant with the two parts swapped. The two variants contain exactly the same tokens. The languages are English, French, Russian and Chinese (verb before object), Hindi, Turkish and Japanese (object before verb), and German (no dominant order). We keep a sentence if:

  • the verb is the first clause-level verb with an object dependent;
  • the object subtree is one contiguous span of at most 6 words without punctuation;
  • the object span is adjacent to the verb with its auxiliaries and particles;
  • neither span starts the sentence, the sentence has 5 to 40 words, and it has no multi-word tokens.

The same detokenizer rebuilds both variants, so their formatting cannot differ.

Intervention and test statistic

We add a multiple of a direction v to the output of block 14, at every position including the sink:

h^{14}_t\;\leftarrow\;h^{14}_t+k\,v,\qquad k\in\{{-2},{-1},{+1},{+2}\}.

The strength k is in units of \lVert\beta^{(-i)}_{\text{OV}}\rVert. For a minimal pair x=(x^{\text{OV}},x^{\text{VO}}) we score both variants under the steered model:

m_k(x)=\log P_k\big(x^{\text{OV}}\big)-\log P_k\big(x^{\text{VO}}\big),\qquad \log P(x)=\sum_{t\ge1}\log P(x_t\mid x_{<t}),\qquad \Delta_k(x)=m_k(x)-m_0(x).

Both variants contain the same tokens. Suppose that a push only makes some words, or the language as a whole, more probable. Then it changes both variants by similar amounts, and the change largely cancels in m. A change in the preferred order does not cancel. The pre-registered statistic is the slope over k=\pm2, computed per language and averaged over the 8 languages with equal weight:

b=\frac1{8}\sum_{i}\frac{\sum_{k=\pm2}k\,\bar\Delta^{(i)}_k}{\sum_{k=\pm2}k^2}=\frac18\sum_i\frac{\bar\Delta^{(i)}_{+2}-\bar\Delta^{(i)}_{-2}}{4},

where \bar\Delta^{(i)}_k is the mean over the pairs of language i. Any push of this size disturbs the model. The null distribution therefore uses 32 random directions of the same norm, drawn in the same space of language differences:

r=\lVert\beta^{(-i)}_{\text{OV}}\rVert\,\frac{U\epsilon}{\lVert U\epsilon\rVert},\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),

with U an orthonormal basis of the span of the 33 centred offsets. The control direction is \beta_{\text{IE}}^{(-i)}, rescaled to the same norm.

Claim rule (pre-registered)

The word-order direction is used if b_{\text{OV}}>0, b_{\text{OV}} exceeds all 32 random slopes (empirical p = 1/33 = 0.03), and z=(b_{\text{OV}}-\overline{b_r})/\mathrm{sd}(b_r)>2.33. Layer 14 is primary. Layers 8 and 20, strengths ±1, the control slope, the sign count over languages and a pair-bootstrap interval are secondary.

Result

The pre-registered claim holds. The slope of the word-order direction is b_{\text{OV}}={+1.52} nats per unit of k (95% pair-bootstrap interval 1.40 to 1.65). The 32 random directions give −0.05 ± 0.32, with a maximum of +1.16. Thus z = 4.99, and the empirical p = 0.03 is the smallest possible with 32 directions. The slope is positive in all 8 languages.

The secondary results agree. The Indo-European direction gives −0.25, inside the range of the random slopes. The dose response is monotone at layers 8, 14 and 20. Over all four strengths, the slopes are 1.06, 1.43 and 1.00 nats per unit of k. The per-language slopes range from 0.88 (Japanese) to 2.96 (Chinese).

Asymmetry and cost (exploratory)

The following analyses were not pre-registered.

  • Asymmetry. A push toward the order that a language does not have moves it far. A push toward the order that it already has moves it little. Chinese moves from −14.1 to −3.5 nats at k = +2, and only to −15.3 at k = −2. Hindi moves from +9.0 to +3.4 at k = −2, and only to +8.9 at k = +2. This is the pattern we expect if the model reads the direction to decide word order and is already confident in the familiar case.
  • Cost. The intervention is not surgical. At k = +2 it lowers the log-probability of the attested sentence by 33.8 nats on average. Random directions of the same norm lower it by 17.1 nats on average (range 1.9 to 95.9 nats).
  • Flips. Unsteered, the model prefers the attested order in 96% of pairs. At k = +2, 9.3% of pairs change their preferred variant toward OV; at k = −2, 10.0% change toward VO. The Indo-European direction at k = +2 also changes 8.8% of pairs, toward VO. Flip counts are therefore not specific; the pre-registered slope is.

This test measures a preference between two strings that we wrote. The model never has to produce either string. The next section asks whether the same push changes the order that the model writes.

Steering free generation

This section asks whether the word-order direction changes the order in which the model itself writes verbs and objects. This test is stronger than the preference test. The model must commit to an order, and each language has its own rules for when an object can come first.

The first pre-registered test could not be evaluated. Exploratory analysis of the same run shows that the direction reorders noun objects in Russian and German, at a strength that also degrades the text. A second, pre-registered test on fresh prompts confirms this in German and Russian. Its test of four new languages could not be evaluated.

Design of the first study (pre-registered)

  • Prompts. 150 FLORES+ devtest sentences in 8 languages: English, German, French, Russian, Hindi, Turkish, Japanese and Chinese. Each prompt is the first 40% of the words of a sentence (of the characters, for Chinese and Japanese). This gives 1,200 prompts with the same content in every language.
  • Generation. Greedy decoding of 40 new tokens, cut at the first newline.
  • Steering. h^{14}_t\leftarrow h^{14}_t+k\,v at every position. The direction v is one of three kinds: the held-out word-order direction of the prompt language, the Indo-European direction, or one of 24 random directions, all at the same norm.
  • Conditions. Unsteered; word-order direction at \pm k^\star and \pm k^\star/2; Indo-European direction at \pm k^\star; 24 random directions at \pm k^\star. These 55 conditions give 66,000 continuations.
  • Strength rule. We calibrated on the first 20 prompts per language with 3 random directions at k\in\{\pm0.5,\pm1,\pm2\}. The rule takes the largest k at which continuations stay in the prompt language at a rate of at least 80% of the unsteered rate. It chose k^\star=2 (88.8% against 98.8% unsteered).
Measurement

A language identifier [14], restricted to the 8 languages, labels each continuation. Only continuations in the prompt language count. This prevents a switch into another language from counting as a change of word order.

Stanza [12] parses each prompt with its continuation. For a continuation x, the set A(x) holds its counted pairs. A counted pair is a dependency with relation \texttt{obj} (any subtype) whose head has the part of speech VERB, with both words in the generated text. Then

n_{\text{OV}}(x)=\big|\{(v,o)\in A(x):\ \mathrm{pos}(o)<\mathrm{pos}(v)\}\big|,\qquad \rho_i(k v)=\frac{\sum_{x\in X_i(kv)}n_{\text{OV}}(x)}{\sum_{x\in X_i(kv)}\lvert A(x)\rvert},

where X_i(kv) holds the continuations of the prompts of language i, steered with kv, that the identifier assigns to language i. We call \rho_i the object-first share; \rho_i(0) is the unsteered share.

The effect of a direction v on language i is the slope of the object-first share in the strength, in share per unit of k:

b_i(v)=\frac{\rho_i(+k^\star v)-\rho_i(-k^\star v)}{2k^\star}.

The pre-registered claim was that the mean of b_i(\beta_{\text{OV}}) over the included languages is positive and exceeds the means of all 24 random directions (empirical p = 1/25 = 0.04), with z>2.33. A language was included if it had at least 20 counted pairs in every one of the 53 conditions that the test needs.

The pre-registered test could not be evaluated

No language met the inclusion rule. Most random directions at k=2 do little harm, but a few push a language almost completely out of itself. In 7 of the 8 languages, between 3 and 9 of the 48 random conditions left fewer than half of the continuations in the prompt language. Chinese had no such random condition, but the word-order direction at k = +2 left only 8% of its continuations in Chinese. Each such condition leaves too few pairs to count. Because the rule required all 53 conditions, it excluded every language.

The design was inconsistent. The strength rule limited the average damage of random directions, but the inclusion rule needed every direction to be harmless. The calibration saw 3 of the 24 directions on 20 prompts per language, too few to show this tail. By the rule that we committed to, this study makes no claim, positive or negative. The next three subsections are exploratory: we decided their analyses after we saw the data.

What the generations show (exploratory)

We decide inclusion separately for each direction. Language i enters the mean for direction v if the unsteered condition and both \pm k^\star v have at least 20 counted pairs:

\bar b(v)=\frac1{\lvert\mathcal L_v\rvert}\sum_{i\in\mathcal L_v}b_i(v),\qquad \mathcal L_v=\Big\{i:\ \min_{k\in\{0,\,+k^\star,\,-k^\star\}}\ \sum_{x\in X_i(kv)}\lvert A(x)\rvert\ \ge 20\Big\}.

For the word-order direction, \mathcal L_v contains five languages: English, French, German, Hindi and Russian. Japanese, Turkish and Chinese drop out. In each of them, the word-order direction, pushed against the language's order, makes the model leave the language. Only 40% of continuations stay in Japanese at k = −2, 55% in Turkish at k = −2, and 8% in Chinese at k = +2.

The pattern matches the preference test (). Each language moves only toward the order that it does not already prefer. Russian, which prefers verb-first, becomes more object-first under +k and does not change under −k (9% unsteered, 8% at −2). German and Hindi, which prefer object-first in these continuations, become more verb-first under −k and change little under +k.

Russian is the clearest case. Its grammar allows an object before the verb for emphasis or style, and the steered continuations remain fluent Russian. German shows a different effect of the same push. Steered toward verb-first, the model writes German with English verb placement, which German grammar does not allow in these clauses. How much of this is reordering? (exploratory)

The object-first share is a ratio. It can change when no object moves, for example when continuations contain fewer verb-first objects. Three checks limit the interpretation.

Object type. Romance languages put unstressed object pronouns before the verb as a rule (French je le vois, "I see it"), and Russian often does. A push that produces more pronouns or fewer nouns raises the share without reordering. We parsed the five included languages again under the unsteered condition, the word-order direction at ±2 and all 48 random conditions. We split each counted pair by the part of speech of the object: noun (NOUN or PROPN), pronoun (PRON) or other. The new parse reproduces the original counts exactly for 94% to 97% of continuations, depending on the language.

German and Russian reorder noun objects. In German, the share of noun objects before their verb falls from 72% to 14% under k = −2. German pronouns stay in front (96% and 92%). In Russian, the noun-object share rises from 1% to 36% under k = +2.

French does not reorder at all: none of its 218 noun objects precedes its verb in any of the three conditions. Its share rises under k = +2 because noun objects almost disappear (6 left, against 100 unsteered). This leaves mostly pronouns, which come before the verb. In Hindi, the share of noun objects before the verb falls from 92% to 53%, but on only 17 pairs. The rest of the Hindi change comes from objects that are neither nouns nor pronouns. These occur only when steered, and 19 of 20 come after the verb.

For noun objects alone, with the same inclusion per direction, English, German and Russian qualify. Their pooled slope is +8.6 points per unit of k. Random directions give +0.3 ± 1.0 (largest +2.8), so z = 8.2.

Text quality. With w_1,\dots,w_n the words of a continuation, its repetition is the share of word bigrams that repeat an earlier bigram:

\mathrm{rep}(x)=1-\frac{\big\lvert\{(w_j,w_{j+1})\}_{j<n}\big\rvert}{n-1}.

Averaged over the five languages, repetition is 4.6% unsteered, 7.5% under the word-order direction, 6.8% under the Indo-European direction and 6.1% under random directions. The mean of a single random condition ranges from 0% to 25%. The word-order push degrades text more than a typical random push of the same norm, and much less than the worst one.

Strength. At k = ±1 the slope is near zero in every language except Hindi. It is +15.7 points per unit of k in Hindi, and at most +1.5 in the others. Except in Hindi, the change appears only at the strength where random directions start to break language identity. The push that reorders is therefore also a push that causes damage.

Language identity is part of the direction (exploratory)

When the word-order direction makes the model leave a language, it does so for one sign of k only. For each language, the sign of k that pushes it away from its own majority order is against, and the other sign is toward.

The destination language varies. Japanese pushed toward verb-first mostly becomes Chinese, and French pushed toward object-first mostly becomes Japanese. But Chinese pushed toward object-first becomes English, so the destination is not simply a language with the target order. We estimated the direction as a difference between groups of languages, so it carries part of what distinguishes those languages as languages. In Japanese, Turkish and Chinese, whose order is fixed, the model more often leaves the language than breaks its order. German is the exception: its verb placement is also fixed by rule, but the model broke the rule and stayed in German.

Verdict of the first study. The pre-registered test did not run, so free-generation steering is not established by our own standard. Exploratory analysis says that the word-order direction changes the order in which the model writes verbs and noun objects in Russian and German. The French effect is an artefact of object type, and the Hindi effect rests on few pairs. The change occurs only at a strength that also degrades the text. A push against a fixed order moves the model out of the language instead of reordering it.

A confirmatory test (pre-registered)

The second study tests the exploratory findings on fresh prompts and on four new languages. The exploratory analysis made three choices after the fact: inclusion per direction, the strength k = 2, and noun objects as the measure. The second study fixes these choices in advance. We committed the pre-registration and the frozen code before any generation.

  • Prompts. 150 FLORES+ devtest sentences that the first study did not use, in 9 languages, each cut after the first 40% of its words: 1,350 prompts. We took them from the same seeded shuffle, continued past its first 150 sentences.
  • Languages with flexible order. In these languages the grammar lets a noun object stand on either side of its verb. German and Dutch put the verb last in subordinate and non-finite clauses, and second in main clauses. Russian, Ukrainian, Polish and Croatian have verb-object basic order with free reordering.
  • Controls with rigid order: English and Spanish (noun objects after the verb) and Korean (verb-final).
  • Directions. Layer 14, estimated without the test language, by the procedure of the preference test. The procedure gives exactly the same German, English and Russian directions as before. For each language, 24 random directions at the same norm.
  • Conditions. Fixed strength k = ±2, without calibration. Unsteered; word-order direction at ±2; Indo-European direction at ±2; 24 random directions at ±2. These 53 conditions give 71,550 continuations.
  • Generation and measurement. Greedy decoding of 40 new tokens, cut at the first newline. The language identifier is restricted to the 9 languages, and Stanza uses the default model of each language. The counting rule is that of the first study. The primary measure is the share of noun objects (NOUN or PROPN) before their verb.

Before any generation, the held-out projections place the new languages as expected: Dutch −0.08, Ukrainian −0.13, Polish −0.21, Croatian −0.16, Spanish −0.35 and Korean +0.52. With n^{\text{N}}_{\text{OV}}(x) and n^{\text{N}}_{\text{VO}}(x) the counted noun-object pairs of a continuation in each order, the primary measure and the slope are

\rho^{\text{N}}_i(kv)=\frac{\sum_{x\in X_i(kv)}n^{\text{N}}_{\text{OV}}(x)}{\sum_{x\in X_i(kv)}\big(n^{\text{N}}_{\text{OV}}(x)+n^{\text{N}}_{\text{VO}}(x)\big)},\qquad b_i(v)=\frac{\rho^{\text{N}}_i(+2v)-\rho^{\text{N}}_i(-2v)}{4}.

The slope b_i(v) is defined only if the unsteered condition, +2v and -2v each have at least 20 noun-object pairs. For a set of languages H, the pooled slope \bar b_H(v) is the mean of the defined b_i(v), i\in H. The null distribution is the pooled slopes of the 24 random directions over the same set, each with its own inclusion. A random direction with no defined language drops out. Then z_H=\big(\bar b_H(\beta_{\text{OV}})-\overline{\bar b_H(r)}\big)/\mathrm{sd}\big(\bar b_H(r)\big).

Hypotheses and claim rule (pre-registered)

H1, replication on fresh prompts: H=\{\text{German},\text{Russian}\}. Evaluable if the word-order direction has a defined slope in both languages.

H2, generalization to new languages: H=\{\text{Dutch},\text{Ukrainian},\text{Polish},\text{Croatian}\}. Evaluable if the word-order direction has a defined slope in at least 3 of the 4 languages.

Each hypothesis also needs a defined pooled slope for at least 20 of the 24 random directions. Claim: the hypothesis is evaluable, \bar b_H(\beta_{\text{OV}}) exceeds every defined random pooled slope, and z_H>2.58 (one-sided 0.005, a Bonferroni correction of 0.01 over the two hypotheses). If a hypothesis is not evaluable, we make no claim and report it as not evaluable.

The secondary predictions carry no claim. Under k = +2, the noun-object share rises in the four Slavic languages. Under k = −2, it falls in German and Dutch. In English, Spanish and Korean, the word-order slope stays inside the central 95% of that language's random slopes. We also report the Indo-European slope and the shares for pronoun objects and for all objects. Further secondary results are the rate of continuations in the prompt language, the sign-specific language loss and the repetition.

The expected effect comes from the first study, where the noun-object slope was +18.1 points per unit of k in German and +7.7 in Russian. We ran a synthetic check with the analysis code, with the exploratory effect sizes for H1 and half of them for H2. Both hypotheses were claimed in 20 of 20 simulations. When the word-order direction behaved like a random direction, the claim rate was 0 of 100 for H1 and 1 of 100 for H2. The pre-registration fixes the reading of the outcomes:

  • H1 and H2 hold: the direction controls the order of noun objects in languages whose grammar allows the choice, at a strength that also costs fluency.
  • H1 holds and H2 fails: the effect is specific to German and Russian, not a general property of languages with flexible order.
  • H1 fails: the exploratory result does not replicate on fresh prompts.

Results of the confirmatory test

This subsection reports the pre-registered result of the second study. The replication (H1) holds. The generalization (H2) could not be evaluated, because two of the four new languages left their language under the push. The run produced the planned 71,550 continuations.

The replication holds. On fresh prompts, the word-order direction changes the order of noun objects in German and Russian. The pooled slope is +15.0 percentage points per unit of k, above all 21 defined random slopes (z = 7.83). The test on four new languages is not evaluable, so it makes no claim. In the two new languages that could be measured, Dutch and Ukrainian, the change is as large (descriptive).

H1, replication (pre-registered: the claim holds). German and Russian both have a defined slope. In German, the share of noun objects before their verb falls from 66% unsteered (68 pairs) to 8% at k = −2 (38 pairs). At k = +2 it is 73%.

In Russian, the share rises from 4% (78 pairs) to 58% at k = +2 (36 pairs); at k = −2 it is 3%. The pooled slope is +15.0 points per unit of k. On average, the share therefore differs by 60 points between k = −2 and k = +2.

Of the 24 random directions, 21 have a defined pooled slope over German and Russian. They give +0.2 ± 1.9 points, with a largest value of +4.2. The word-order slope exceeds all of them, with z = 7.83. The empirical p-value is 1/22 = 0.045, the smallest possible value with 21 random directions. Per language, z is 4.97 in German and 16.7 in Russian. The Indo-European control gives −0.8 points.

A prompt bootstrap, which we did not pre-register, puts the 95% interval of the pooled slope at +11.8 to +18.2 points. The German change is similar to that of the first study (72% to 14% there). The Russian change is larger (1% to 36% there).

H2, generalization (pre-registered: not evaluable). Only Dutch and Ukrainian have a defined slope. The rule required 3 of the 4 languages, so H2 makes no claim. Polish and Croatian drop out at k = +2, the push toward object-first, which is against their majority order. Only 27% of Polish and 37% of Croatian continuations stay in the prompt language. This leaves 2 and 17 noun-object pairs, against the minimum of 20.

Most of the other Polish continuations are English, Croatian or German, and most of the other Croatian continuations are English or German. The steered text in both languages also repeats more. The share of repeated word bigrams under the word-order direction is 14.4% in Polish and 16.0% in Croatian, against 3.8% and 5.3% unsteered. This is the failure that the first study showed: pushed against its order, the model leaves the language instead of reordering it.

Descriptively, the two measurable languages move as strongly as German and Russian. In Dutch, the noun-object share falls from 70% (91 pairs) to 10% at k = −2 (51 pairs); per-language z = 4.94. In Ukrainian, it rises from 3% (62 pairs) to 54% at k = +2 (26 pairs); z = 12.3. Pooled over these two languages, the slope is +15.0 points per unit of k (prompt bootstrap +8.3 to +18.7). The largest random pooled slope is +4.0. These numbers describe the data; they are not a pre-registered test.

Controls and secondary results (pre-registered, no claim).

  • Rigid controls. In English and Spanish, the noun-object share stays between 0% and 1% in all three conditions. As predicted, the slope is inside the central 95% of the random slopes. In Korean, the share is 100% unsteered and at k = +2. At k = −2, only 71% of continuations stay Korean and no noun-object pair is counted, so the Korean prediction cannot be checked.
  • Directional predictions. All four that can be checked hold. The share falls under k = −2 in German and Dutch, and rises under k = +2 in Russian and Ukrainian.
  • Indo-European control. −0.8 points per unit of k over the H1 languages and +0.9 over the H2 languages.
  • Sign-specific language loss. Continuations stay in the prompt language in 76% of cases when the push is against the language's order. They stay in 95% of cases when it is toward it. The rate is lower against in 7 of 9 languages, but the sign test gives p = 0.18. The difference that the first study found (p = 0.016, exploratory) is therefore not confirmed at the 0.05 level.
  • Random directions. In every language, between 6 and 18 of the 48 random conditions have fewer than 20 noun-object pairs. A rule that needs every condition, as in the first study, would therefore again have excluded every language. Inclusion per direction kept H1 evaluable.

The pre-registered readings did not include this outcome: H1 holds, and H2 could not be tested.

Verdict of the confirmatory test. The word-order direction changes the order in which Qwen2.5-7B writes noun objects in German and Russian, on prompts that it had not seen. The exploratory finding is confirmed. Generalization to other languages with flexible order is not established. Dutch and Ukrainian show the same large change, but only descriptively, and Polish and Croatian left their language when pushed against their order. All effects need the strength k = 2, at which some random directions also push a language out of itself.

A word-order direction from inside each language

This section estimates a word-order direction from pairs of sentences that differ only in word order, inside each language. It shows three pre-registered results. The within-language direction is aligned with the between-language word-order direction in the middle layers. Steering along it reorders noun objects in German and Dutch. Unlike the between-language direction, it keeps the model in the prompt language.

Why a second direction

The word-order direction \beta_{\text{OV}} is a difference between groups of languages. It therefore can carry part of what makes those languages different as languages. The free-generation studies show this. Pushed against their own order, Japanese, Turkish and Chinese (first study) and Polish and Croatian (confirmatory study) often left their language.

A direction estimated from pairs of sentences in the same language cannot carry language identity, because both sentences of a pair are in one language. We call it the within-language direction d_W. The study asks three questions, each pre-registered with a claim (commit 14fa22e):

  1. A, alignment. Does d_W point along \beta_{\text{OV}}?
  2. B, reordering. Does steering along d_W change the order of noun objects?
  3. C, retention. Does d_W keep the model in the prompt language better than \beta_{\text{OV}} does?

The within-language direction

We use the 999 minimal pairs of the preference test, in 8 languages. Each pair holds an attested sentence and its twin with the verb and its object swapped, with the same tokens. For each sentence we take the mean-pooled residual stream over the content tokens, at every layer. This is the same pooling as for the language centroids. For pair p, let

\Delta_p=\bar h\big(x^{\text{OV}}_p\big)-\bar h\big(x^{\text{VO}}_p\big).

The difference mixes two effects: the change of order, and the change from an attested sentence to a constructed one, which is less natural. If the attested order is VO, the OV twin is the constructed one; if it is OV, the VO twin is. We separate the two effects by weighted least squares, per layer and per coordinate:

\Delta_p=d_W+s_p\,d_U+e_p,\qquad s_p=\begin{cases}+1&\text{attested order VO}\\-1&\text{attested order OV}\end{cases},\qquad w_p=\frac{1}{n_{\ell(p)}} .

Here d_U takes the unnaturalness of the constructed twin, and d_W is the within-language word-order direction. The weight w_p divides by the number of pairs of the pair's language \ell(p), so that every language counts equally.

  • Validation. With planted order and unnaturalness vectors and the real pair counts, the estimator recovers both (cosine 0.998 and 1.000). A plain mean of the differences does not (cosine 0.92 with the order vector, 0.46 with the unnaturalness vector). At layer 14 the two estimated components have a cosine of 0.54, so a plain mean would mix them.
  • Held-out versions. For claim A we fit d_W on all 8 pair languages. For steering German, Russian and English, which are pair languages, we fit it without that language's pairs.
  • Reliability. The cosine between estimates from two halves of the pairs is 0.85 to 0.94 at layers 2 to 28, and 0.54 at layer 1.
  • Layer 0. The two twins contain the same tokens, so their mean-pooled embeddings are identical and d_W is zero up to rounding. Layer 0 carries no information in this study.
  • Norm. At layer 14 the natural norm of d_W is 1.24. For steering, we rescale it to \lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert of the prompt language, about 9.4 times larger, so that both directions have the same norm.

A. Alignment (pre-registered: holds)

At layer 14 we compare d_W with \beta_{\text{OV}} estimated from all 34 languages. The null distribution uses 10,000 random unit vectors, uniform in the 33-dimensional span of the 34 centred dev centroids. This is the space in which \beta_{\text{OV}} lies. The claim needs a positive cosine above the 99.9th percentile of the null.

The cosine is +0.232, against a 99.9th percentile of +0.206 (null standard deviation 0.071; p = 0.0002). The claim holds. Most of d_W lies outside the span of language differences: the span holds 16% of its squared norm. Inside the span, the cosine with \beta_{\text{OV}} is +0.58, and

\cos(d_W,\beta_{\text{OV}})=\sqrt{\tfrac{\lVert P d_W\rVert^2}{\lVert d_W\rVert^2}}\;\cos(P d_W,\beta_{\text{OV}})\approx\sqrt{0.16}\times0.58\approx0.23,

where P projects onto the span. The unnaturalness component is not aligned: \cos(d_U,\beta_{\text{OV}})={+0.003}.

The Indo-European control is not near zero. The pre-registration expected \cos(d_W,\beta_{\text{IE}}) near 0. It is −0.18 at layer 14, about 2.5 null standard deviations below zero, and between −0.15 and −0.24 at layers 6 to 18. This expectation therefore did not hold.

We give one possible reason, which we did not test. In our sample, OV order and Indo-European membership are negatively associated: 4 of 16 Indo-European languages are OV, against 7 of 18 others. In the held-out fits used for steering, \beta_{\text{OV}} and \beta_{\text{IE}} themselves are almost orthogonal (cosine −0.03 to +0.02).

B. Reordering (pre-registered: holds)

We repeat the confirmatory free-generation run with d_W: the same 1,350 prompts in 9 languages, greedy decoding, steering at block 14 at every position. The conditions are unsteered; d_W at k\in\{\pm1,\pm2,\pm3\}; and \beta_{\text{OV}} at k=\pm2, run again for a paired comparison. The measure and the inclusion rule are those of the confirmatory study: noun-object share, and at least 20 noun-object pairs unsteered, at +2 and at −2.

  • Statistic. The pooled slope \bar b(d_W): the mean of b_i(d_W)=\big(\rho^{\text{N}}_i(+2d_W)-\rho^{\text{N}}_i(-2d_W)\big)/4 over the included languages of the flexible set (German, Dutch, Russian, Ukrainian, Polish, Croatian).
  • Null. The 24 random directions of the confirmatory study, pooled over the same set, each with its own inclusion.
  • Claim. At least 3 languages included for d_W, at least 20 random directions defined, \bar b(d_W) above every random pooled slope, and z>2.58.

The unsteered and \beta_{\text{OV}} continuations are 100% identical to those of the confirmatory study, because greedy decoding is deterministic here. The random null from that study is therefore exact for this run.

All 6 flexible languages are included. The pooled slope is +3.5 percentage points per unit of k. The 24 random directions give −0.1 ± 0.8, with a largest value of +1.7. The slope exceeds all of them, with z = 4.56 (empirical p = 1/25 = 0.04). The claim holds.

Two languages carry the effect: German (+10.3 points per unit of k) and Dutch (+8.5). The Slavic slopes are near zero: Croatian +1.5, Ukrainian +0.6, Russian +0.1 and Polish 0.0. In German and Dutch the change goes both ways:

C. Language retention (pre-registered: holds)

For each of the 9 languages, the against sign of k pushes the language away from its own majority order. At the against sign and k = 2, we compare the share of continuations in the prompt language under d_W and under \beta_{\text{OV}}, in the same run. The claim needs claim B and a higher share for d_W in at least 8 of the 9 languages.

Under d_W, 98% of continuations stay in the prompt language on average, against 76% under \beta_{\text{OV}}. The share is higher for d_W in 8 of the 9 languages, and Dutch ties (99% under both). The sign test over the 8 languages that differ gives p = 0.008. The claim holds. The largest differences are in Polish (98% against 27%), Croatian (95% against 37%) and Korean (100% against 71%).

The text also degrades less. At k = +2, the share of repeated word bigrams is 5.2% in Polish and 9.3% in Croatian under d_W. Under \beta_{\text{OV}} it is 24.8% and 26.0%, and unsteered it is 3.8% and 5.3%.

All three pre-registered claims hold. The word-order direction that the model uses inside each language lies along the between-language word-order axis in the middle layers (cosine +0.23, p = 0.0002). Steering along it reorders noun objects (pooled over six flexible-order languages, z = 4.56; the effect comes from German and Dutch) and keeps the model in the prompt language (98% against 76%).

How much of the between-language effect runs through the within-language direction? (exploratory)

The following argument uses arithmetic that we did after seeing the data. Both directions are scaled to \lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert. A push of k\,\beta_{\text{OV}} therefore contains a component along d_W equal to a push along d_W of strength

k_{\text{eff}}=k\,\cos(\beta_{\text{OV}},d_W),\qquad \text{component}=k\,\cos(\beta_{\text{OV}},d_W)\,\lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert .

Per flexible language, the cosine of the steering d_W with \beta_{\text{OV}} is 0.20 to 0.23, so at k = 2 we get k_{\text{eff}} = 0.40 to 0.47. Even at the larger strength k = ±1, d_W has no clear effect. For example, German is at 71%, 66% and 70% at k = −1, 0 and +1, and Russian at 7%, 4% and 5%. Yet \beta_{\text{OV}} at k = −2 moves German to 8%, and at k = +2 Russian to 58%.

Assume that the effects of the components add without interaction; we did not test this. Then most of the effect of \beta_{\text{OV}} on word order runs through components orthogonal to d_W. This agrees with two exploratory observations. \beta_{\text{OV}} makes the model write German with English verb placement, and it moves Polish and Croatian out of the language. In contrast, d_W reorders inside the language.

What this means, and its limits

The between-language word-order axis contains the model's own within-language word-order feature, in the middle layers. Steering along that feature reorders noun objects in German and Dutch without a change of language. By the exploratory argument above, the extra Slavic reordering of \beta_{\text{OV}} and its language switching probably come from components that d_W does not share. Several limits apply:

  • All states are mean-pooled over the sentence, so d_W is a sentence-level summary, not a per-token feature.
  • For steering, d_W is about 9.4 times its natural norm at layer 14. The strengths that reorder are large for this direction.
  • The pairs come from 8 languages. Of the 6 flexible-order generation languages, only German and Russian have pairs.
  • One model (Qwen2.5-7B), one steering layer (14), and an alignment cosine of 0.23: most of d_W lies outside the span of language differences.
  • The Indo-European control did not behave as expected, and we do not know why.

Language identity, token by token

So far each sentence was one point. This section follows the representation of Qwen2.5-7B token by token, with two pre-registered tests. The first passes: when the evidence about the language is ambiguous, the representation encodes graded uncertainty. The second fails: at a code switch, the representation moves to the new language within about one token. A Bayesian observer of the whole sequence moves much more slowly.

Transformers can linearly represent belief states over the hidden variables of the process that generated their training data [6]. The language of a text is such a hidden variable. The hypothesis is that the model keeps a probability distribution over the language that it reads, and updates it with each token. Mean-pooled sentences hide this, so we recorded the residual stream at every token.

Data and the model's own belief

  • Text. FLORES+ devtest in the 12 pilot languages, every content position, at 15 layers (0 to 28 in steps of 2). We split the sentences into a fit half and a test half. All maps are ridge regressions, with the penalty chosen by 5-fold cross-validation on the fit half.
  • Coordinates. Each token state is reduced to its coordinates in the 11-dimensional span S of the 12 language offsets: z_t=S^\top h^\ell_t.
  • Belief. Let \tau_j be the prompt "The following text is written in language j." We estimate the model's own posterior over the 12 languages with a uniform prior, on the first 24 content tokens of each sentence:
\pi_t(j)\ \propto\ \exp\Big(\sum_{t'=1}^{t}\log P\big(x_{t'}\mid\tau_j,\,x_{<t'}\big)\Big).

Graded belief (pre-registered: supported)

If the representation encodes belief, an affine map from the posterior, z_t\approx W\pi_t+c, predicts it better than an affine map from the hard decision e_{\arg\max\pi_t}. We test this on held-out sentences at ambiguous positions, where \max_j\pi_t(j)<0.9. The statistic is the difference in mean squared error, averaged over layers 8 to 16, with a one-sided paired bootstrap over test sentences (10,000 resamples) and a threshold of 0.025.

The posterior wins: the error difference is 55.6 (p = 0.0001) on 26,435 ambiguous test positions. It wins at every layer; at layer 14 the errors are 147.1 (posterior) and 203.6 (hard decision). Two secondary results qualify this:

  • The posterior adds information beyond the current token. A baseline from the identity and position of the current token has error 102.4 at layer 14; adding the posterior lowers it to 84.7. The posterior improves this baseline at every layer from 2 to 28.
  • The true language label predicts the representation better than the posterior (115.4 at layer 14). The internal state is therefore more certain than our estimate of the model's belief.

Bayesian switching (pre-registered: not supported)

We built 28,800 code-switched sequences: the first part of a sentence in language A, followed by the rest of the same sentence in language B. They cover 8 pairs of languages in both orders, 200 sentences and 9 switch points (after 10% to 90% of the words, or of the characters for Chinese and Japanese). With \bar z_A and \bar z_B the mean coordinates of natural text in A and B, the position of each token on the line between the two languages is

\lambda_t=\frac{(\bar z_B-\bar z_A)^\top(z_t-\bar z_A)}{\lVert\bar z_B-\bar z_A\rVert^2},

which is 0 at A and 1 at B. A Bayesian observer of the whole sequence predicts \lambda_t\approx a+b\,\sigma(\mathrm{LLR}_t), with

\mathrm{LLR}_t=\sum_{t'\le t}\Big[\log P(x_{t'}\mid\tau_B,x_{<t'})-\log P(x_{t'}\mid\tau_A,x_{<t'})\Big].

This curve rises slowly after the switch, because the evidence for A that accumulated before the switch must first be overcome. A step, \lambda_t\approx a+b\,\mathbb 1[t\ge t_{\text{switch}}], predicts an immediate jump. The pre-registered claim needed two results, each at α = 0.025, averaged over layers 8, 12, 14 and 16:

  1. The Bayesian curve has a lower held-out error than the step.
  2. The lag increases with the length of the prefix in A. The lag is the number of tokens after the switch until \lambda_t>0.5.

Result 1 fails. The step fits much better: at layer 14 the held-out errors are 0.117 (step) and 0.250 (Bayesian curve). For the pre-registered one-sided test of the difference, p = 1.0. Result 2 holds: the lag increases with the prefix length (Spearman ρ = +0.13, p = 0.0005). Because the claim needed both, Bayesian switching is not supported.

The mean lag at layer 14 is 1.24 tokens (0.79 to 1.67 over the layers). In an exploratory follow-up, we let the observer forget. The evidence decays with a half-life of H tokens, L_H(t)=\sum_{t'\le t}2^{-(t-t')/H}\,d_{t'}, where d_{t'} is the term in brackets above. The best fit is H=8 at every tested layer, and it is still much worse than the step: 0.218 against 0.117 at layer 14.

The representation therefore behaves like an estimate of the language of the current stretch of text. It is graded when that stretch is ambiguous, and it has a small inertia that grows with the length of the preceding passage.

Both tests use the prompt-based posterior, which is a noisy estimate of the model's belief: the true label predicts the representation better than the posterior does. This noise weakens every test that uses the posterior.

Discussion and limitations

This section summarizes what the results show, states their limits, and lists the lessons for method.

What the results show

Two structures that linguists use to classify languages are present in how these models arrange languages. They occupy different depths. The family tree is strongest in the layers that read and write tokens. The word-order axis is strongest in the middle layers. The two structures replicate in two models with different tokenizers, data and training runs.

The word-order axis is the more informative of the two. No single token shows it, so it must come from how the model processes the arrangement of tokens. Steering along it changes the model's preference between the two orders of the same words in eight languages from four families. It also changes the order that the model writes, at least in German and Russian. Exploratory analysis found that it reorders noun objects in these two languages, and a pre-registered replication on fresh prompts confirmed it (z = 7.83).

Whether this holds for other languages with flexible order is open. The pre-registered test on four new languages could not be evaluated. Dutch and Ukrainian changed as much as German and Russian, but Polish and Croatian left their language when pushed against their order. For the between-language direction, the effect needs a strength at which the push also degrades text or moves the model out of the language.

A pre-registered follow-up separates these effects. A direction estimated inside languages, from sentence pairs that differ only in order, lies along the word-order axis in the middle layers. Steering along it reorders noun objects in German and Dutch, and the model stays in the prompt language. The between-language direction therefore contains the model's own word-order feature, and also other components that carry language identity. An exploratory argument suggests that these other components cause most of the Slavic reordering and the language switching of the between-language direction.

Limitations

  • Presence, not emergence. We show that the structures are present in two trained models. We do not show how they emerge during training; models with released training checkpoints can answer this.
  • Few languages break the bundle. Only Persian, Finnish and Estonian separate object–verb order from adposition order, so the separation of the head-direction features rests on three languages.
  • A blunt intervention. At k = +2 the push costs 33.8 nats on the attested sentence. Our direction is defined by object–verb order alone, and we tested it only in Qwen2.5-7B, with layer 14 as the primary layer.
  • One text collection. All representations are means over FLORES+ sentences, which are translations of the same source sentences.
  • Training data. We did not control for the amount of training data in each language. Tokens per sentence, which is a covariate, tracks it only in part.
  • Few features. We tested four word-order features of the many that WALS describes.
  • Free generation. The confirmed effect covers noun objects in two languages, one model, one layer and one strength (k = 2). The generalization to other languages rests on descriptive results in Dutch and Ukrainian.
  • Within-language direction. It comes from mean-pooled states of minimal pairs in 8 languages. For steering we scaled it to about 9.4 times its natural norm. The Indo-European control did not behave as expected, and the reason is open.
  • Token level. The token-level study uses one model, 12 languages and a noisy estimate of the model's belief.

Lessons for method

Geometric claims about language representations are easy to fool. Each artefact below produced, or nearly produced, a false result in this project:

  • Per-language PCA creates an apparent failure of maps to compose.
  • Mean pooling creates a scale factor per language that follows the token count.
  • Shared tokens and shared characters can look like genealogy; the controls must remove them.
  • Marginal tests of overlapping structures leak into each other; conditional tests separate them.
  • A ratio such as the object-first share can change without reordering, when the mix of object types changes.
  • A direction estimated between groups of languages also carries language identity. A direction from minimal pairs inside each language does not, and it separates word order from language.
  • An inclusion rule must be consistent with the strength rule; otherwise a pre-registered test can fail to run. Inclusion per direction made the replication evaluable, but a minimum number of languages still left the generalization test without an answer.

Each pre-registered positive result in this article survived a test designed in advance to rule out such artefacts. Two of our own early findings, the failure of maps to compose and the French free-generation effect, did not survive the checks.

Extraction

  • Forward hooks on the embedding module and on every decoder block capture the residual stream. We do not use the last hidden state of the Hugging Face output, because it already includes the final norm.
  • Pooling happens inside each hook, so no full sequence of activations is kept across layers. We store the pooled activations in float16. The models run in bfloat16 without quantization.
  • Batches are formed by length and limited by a budget of padded tokens. A batch that runs out of memory is split.
  • Every input starts with the sink token (BOS for Llama, a newline for Qwen), and pooling drops position 0.
  • Fertility f_i is the mean number of content tokens per sentence over both halves. The token distance uses the token-frequency distributions of the dev and devtest text, with each model's own tokenizer.

Geometries, distances and the split regression

  • Causal geometry. The covariance of the unembedding rows uses the normalization N_V-1 over the real vocabulary (rows with an index below 151,665 for Qwen and below 128,000 for Llama). \omega is the weight of the final RMSNorm.
  • Whitened geometry. W is computed per layer on the dev half. The per-layer profiles also use the shrinkage \eta=0.1 and the Euclidean inner product.
  • Bootstrap. 50 resamples of sentences with multinomial weights. The same sentence indices apply to every language, and the window mean is recomputed for each resample.
  • Rescaled pooling. Each sentence vector is multiplied by \sqrt{T_{i,s}/\bar T}, where T_{i,s} is its number of content tokens and \bar T the mean over all languages and both halves. The tests then use 2,000 permutations.
  • Fit. Non-negative least squares on the 561 values of D^2_{ij}. The columns are the 34 star indicators, the 4 script splits, the two covariates divided by their standard deviations, and the splits under test. R^2=1-\mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}.
  • Permutations. For the family tree, a random permutation of the 34 language labels maps every split of \mathcal T to a new split of the same size and the same nesting. For a single split, the null draws random subsets of the same size. A draw counts as at least as large if G_b\ge G_{\text{obs}}-10^{-12}.
  • Same-language test. Residuals of the fit with the star and the two covariates only. Each pair receives its percentile rank among the 561 residuals (0 = closest). The statistic is the mean rank of the three pairs; the p-value counts random triples of pairs with a mean rank at least as small.
  • Power. Before data collection we simulated Brownian motion on the Glottolog tree with realistic leaf lengths and script structure. With 34 languages and internal branches that carry 10% of the distance, the family-tree test has a power of 80% to 86%; with the 12 pilot languages, 18% to 42%.
  • Exploratory robustness. 2,000 permutations. Geography is the great-circle distance between the WALS coordinates of two languages, added as a covariate.

Typology and lexical controls

  • The WALS values come from the WALS data in CLDF format. Uncoded values take the value of the genus: Kazakh for 83A, 85A and 86A, Azerbaijani for 85A, and Maltese for 87A.
  • Character overlap: transliteration with unidecode, lower case, sets of character trigrams, Jaccard index per parallel sentence, mean over the dev and devtest sentences.
  • ASJP: the 40-item lists. The word lists were fixed in advance; Serbian uses the Serbo-Croatian list, both Chinese scripts use the Mandarin list, and Filipino uses the Tagalog list. For synonyms, LDN takes the minimum over the forms.
  • Tests A1 and A2 use random subsets of 11 and 12 languages; B1 and B2 use the leaf-permutation null. 10,000 permutations each.

Steering with minimal pairs

  • Directions. Ordinary least squares on the dev centroid offsets in the raw coordinates of the residual stream, at layers 8, 14 and 20, once for each held-out test language. The Indo-European direction is rescaled to \lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert. The 32 random directions are Gaussian vectors in the span of the 33 centred offsets, normalized and scaled to \lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert, with a fixed seed.
  • Pairs. The test sets of the Universal Dependencies treebanks English EWT, German GSD, French GSD, Russian SynTagRus, Hindi HDTB, Turkish IMST, Japanese GSD and Chinese GSD, at most 150 pairs per language.
  • Scoring. The sum of the log-probabilities of the content tokens given the sink. The steering vector is added to the output of the block at every position, including the sink.
  • Conditions. Unsteered; the word-order and Indo-European directions at layers 8, 14 and 20 with k\in\{\pm1,\pm2\}; the 32 random directions at layer 14 with k=\pm2.
  • Statistics. \mathrm{sd}(b_r) uses the normalization n-1. The empirical p-value is (1+\#\{r:b_r\ge b_{\text{OV}}\})/33. The 95% interval of b_{\text{OV}} comes from 5,000 bootstrap resamples of pairs within each language.

Free generation

  • Prompts. A seeded shuffle of the 1,012 devtest indices; the first study takes the first 150, and the confirmatory study takes the next 150 that the first study did not use. A prompt keeps the first 40% of the words (at least 3 words), or for Chinese and Japanese the first 40% of the characters (at least 6).
  • Language identification. langid.py [14], restricted to the study languages; a continuation shorter than 3 characters counts as no language.
  • Parsing. Stanza with tokenization, multi-word-token expansion where the language has it, part-of-speech tagging, lemmatization and dependency parsing, applied to prompt and continuation together. A word counts as generated if it starts after the end of the prompt.
  • Exploratory statistics. The prompt bootstrap uses 2,000 resamples of the 150 sentence indices. The re-parse by object type ran on the unsteered condition, the word-order and Indo-European directions at ±2 and all 48 random conditions.
  • Confirmatory run. A debug run on 2 prompts per language with 1 random direction tested the pipeline first; its numbers are not used. The frozen analysis code (analysis_gen2.py) contains the synthetic check of power and false-positive rate and produced results_gen2.json.
  • Confirmatory, descriptive additions. The prompt bootstrap of the pooled slopes (bootstrap_gen2.py, not pre-registered) draws the 150 sentences with replacement 2,000 times. Each draw applies to every language and condition and keeps the inclusion rule. The destination languages of Polish and Croatian continuations come from the language-identifier labels of the generated text.

Within-language direction

  • States. The 999 minimal pairs of the preference test, with the sink token prepended. Mean pooling over the content tokens with the sink dropped, at every layer, in the same way as for the language centroids (pairstates.py).
  • Estimator. Weighted least squares per layer and coordinate, with an intercept (d_W), the sign term s_p (d_U) and weights 1/n_{\ell(p)}. The synthetic check used planted vectors and the real pair counts. Split-half reliability uses a random half of the pairs in each language.
  • Alignment. \beta_{\text{OV}} and \beta_{\text{IE}} from the regression of the preference test, fitted on all 34 languages. Null: 10,000 Gaussian vectors in an orthonormal basis of the span of the 34 centred dev centroids, normalized. p=(1+\#\{\text{null}\ge\cos\})/10{,}001.
  • Generation. The 1,350 prompts of the confirmatory study, greedy decoding of 40 tokens, block 14, every position. Nine conditions: unsteered, d_W at \pm1,\pm2,\pm3, and \beta_{\text{OV}} at \pm2. The random null comes from the confirmatory study; its unsteered and \beta_{\text{OV}} continuations are identical to this run.
  • Retention test. The against sign uses the unsteered noun-object share (above 0.5 means object-first; all objects if a language has no noun pair). Two-sided sign test; languages with equal rates are left out.
  • Debug run. 3 pairs and 2 prompts per language tested the pipeline first; its numbers are not used.

Token-level study

  • Each token state is projected onto the 11-dimensional span of the language offsets (dev centroids) and onto the top 117 principal components of the token states orthogonal to that span. The tests use the span coordinates.
  • The tag-conditioned log-likelihoods cover the first 24 content tokens of every sentence (283,900 positions). Ambiguous positions have \max_j\pi_t(j)<0.9.
  • The baseline for token identity uses the layer-0 embedding of the current token, projected to the span, plus a one-hot code of the position.
  • The code-switched sequences use the states at 8 layers and the log-likelihoods under the tags of A and B. The lag is censored at the length of the B segment.

Compute

The GPU jobs ran on Google Colab, on L4 GPUs. Extraction for one model and 34 languages takes about 30 minutes; the whole project used about 10 L4 GPU-hours. The confirmatory free-generation run used 2.76 Colab compute units, and the within-language study 0.69.
