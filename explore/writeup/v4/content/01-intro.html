Languages as points

This section states the question, the prior work, and the terms that we use in the rest of the article.

Interpretability research often finds that a model represents a familiar object with a shape that matches the structure of the object. Days of the week and months lie on circles. Numbers lie on helices. When a model counts the characters on a line, the count lies on a curved manifold. Attention heads rotate this manifold to compare the count with the line width [1].

Languages also have structure. Linguists classify them in two independent ways. The first is descent: languages with a common ancestor form a family, and each family forms a tree. For example, Indo-European has Germanic, Romance and Slavic branches. The second is type: languages differ in properties such as the order of the verb and its object, independently of their ancestry.

English (VO) The dog chased the ball. (verb before object)
Turkish (OV) Köpek topu kovaladı. (dog ball chased: object before verb)

A model that learns from text in many languages never sees a label for either property. We ask four questions about the arrangement of languages inside the model:

  1. Does the arrangement contain the family tree?
  2. Does it contain a word-order structure, separately from the family tree?
  3. At which depth does each structure occur?
  4. Does the model use the word-order structure when it processes text?

Earlier work shows that multilingual representations have a language-neutral part and a language-specific part. Subtracting the mean representation of each language improves cross-lingual retrieval [2][3]. Language means cluster loosely by family [3], and family trees can be recovered from the output distributions of models [8]. Decoder models process many languages through shared middle-layer features [4][7]. We know of no earlier test that separates descent from type and from surface overlap, such as shared script and shared tokens. This article gives such a test for decoder models, across depth, with a causal check.

Terms used in this article

Layer ℓ: the residual stream after block ℓ; layer 0 is the output of the embedding. Depth: ℓ divided by the number of blocks.

Centroid and offset: the mean representation of a language, and the centroid minus the mean of all 34 centroids.

Geometry: the inner product with which we measure distances between offsets (causal or whitened, defined in Setup).

Split: a division of the 34 languages into two groups. Family tree: the 20 splits of the Glottolog tree. Word-order split: the 11 object-before-verb (OV) languages against the 23 others.

Gain: the fraction of the remaining squared-distance error that a set of splits removes (defined in Testing for a tree and an axis).

Axis: the word-order structure in the arrangement of the languages. Direction: a vector in the residual stream that we add to the model to steer it. Strength k: the multiple of a direction that we add.
