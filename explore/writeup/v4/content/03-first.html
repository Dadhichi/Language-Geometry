A first look: translations and a trap

This section reports a pilot study that shaped the design of the main study. It shows three things. Languages differ mostly by a constant offset. Mean pooling adds a scale factor per language. Per-language principal component analysis (PCA) creates a false result. All results in this section are exploratory.

The pilot used Qwen2.5-7B and every second layer from 0 to 28. Its 12 languages are English, German, French, Spanish, Russian, Hindi, Arabic, Chinese, Japanese, Turkish, Vietnamese and Indonesian. Four independent analyses used the same activations. We validated every statistic on synthetic data that we sent through the same pipeline.

Languages differ mostly by an offset

To a good approximation, a sentence representation in the middle layers is the sum of four terms:

\bar h^{\ell}(x_{i,s})\;\approx\;\mu_0+a_i+\kappa_i\,\psi_s+\varepsilon_{i,s}.

Here \mu_0 is a common mean and a_i the offset of language i. The vector \psi_s is the content of sentence s that all its translations share. The scalar \kappa_i is a scale factor per language, and \varepsilon_{i,s} is the remainder. The terms differ in size:

TermDescriptionSize in the pilot
Offset a_ione constant vector per languageremoves 82–91% of the cross-language error that a linear map can remove, at every layer
Scale factor \kappa_ione scalar per language\log\kappa_i correlates with \log f_i at −0.92 to −0.96, at every layer
Rotationa small rotation per pair of languages2–4% of the variance in the middle layers, about 15% at layer 26; the rotations do not commute

The offset alone is enough to match translations. We subtract each language's centroid and match each sentence to the most similar sentence of the other language by cosine similarity:

\hat s=\arg\max_{s'}\ \cos\big(\bar h(x_{i,s})-\mu_i,\ \bar h(x_{j,s'})-\mu_j\big).

At layer 14, this finds the correct translation among the 1,012 devtest sentences in 99.1% of cases, averaged over all ordered pairs of languages. The centroids come from the dev half.

The scale factor comes from mean pooling

The scale factor is a property of the pooling, not of the model. With f_i the mean number of tokens per sentence,

\log\kappa_i\;\approx\;c-\tfrac12\log f_i .

This is the scaling of a mean of weakly correlated vectors. Suppose that the part of each token state that carries sentence content varies from token to token with little correlation. Then the norm of its mean over T tokens falls as T^{-1/2}. Multiplying each sentence vector by \sqrt{T} removes 80% of the variance of \log\kappa_i. Every analysis below therefore includes \lvert\log f_i-\log f_j\rvert as a covariate, and we also report a variant with rescaled pooling.

A trap: maps that seem not to compose

The pilot also gave a false result, and we report it because the mistake is easy to make. We first fitted orthogonal maps between languages, each inside the top-q principal subspace of its own language. Let X_i hold the sentence representations of language i as rows, B_i\in\mathbb R^{d\times q} its top-q principal directions, and Z_i=(X_i-\mu_i)B_i. Then

R_{ij}=\arg\min_{R\in O(q)}\lVert Z_iR-Z_j\rVert_F,\qquad E_{ijm}=\frac{\lVert Z_i\,(R_{ij}R_{jm}-R_{im})\rVert_F^2}{\lVert Z_i\rVert_F^2}.

If one shared representation relates the languages, the maps compose and the composition error E_{ijm} is near 0. Against a null simulation built on this assumption, the real error was 1.5 to 4.5 times too large.

The cause is the truncation to q dimensions. Suppose that the languages are exactly consistent: X_j-\mu_j=(X_i-\mu_i)\,Q_{ij} with orthogonal maps that compose, Q_{ij}Q_{jm}=Q_{im}. The best q\times q map is close to the orthogonal part of T_{ij}=B_i^\top Q_{ij}B_j, and

T_{ij}\,T_{jm}=B_i^\top Q_{ij}\,P_j\,Q_{jm}B_m,\qquad P_j=B_jB_j^\top .

This product equals T_{im}=B_i^\top Q_{im}B_m only if the projector P_j acts as the identity on the directions involved. That is true only if the maps carry the three subspaces exactly onto one another. For subspaces estimated from about 1,000 sentences, this is false: the top-64 subspaces of different languages overlap only partly (mean squared cosine about 0.64). The composition test therefore measured subspace mismatch. Two checks confirm this:

  • Exactly consistent synthetic data, sent through the same pipeline, give E=0.091 at layer 14 with q=64. The real data give 0.077. The original null model predicted 0.017.
  • When all languages use one shared subspace, B_j:=Q_{ij}^\top B_i, the real maps compose as well as consistent synthetic data do (0.0016 against 0.0020).

For this reason, the rest of the article uses distances between centroids, which need no basis per language.
