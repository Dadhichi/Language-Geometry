The residual map

This section shows the arrangement of the 34 languages after we remove the effects that we expect from surface properties. The first coordinate of the result separates the languages by word order. The map is descriptive; the next section tests what it shows.

Most of the variation in the squared distances is a star. In a star, the squared distance between two languages is close to the sum of two terms, one for each language. Each term is the distance of that language from the centre, and it depends mostly on the number of tokens per sentence. Languages with long tokenizations, such as Telugu and Khmer, are far from the centre. In Qwen, the star alone explains 52% of the variation of D^2 in the causal geometry and 75% in the whitened geometry. We construct the map () as follows:

  1. Compute D^2 from the window mean \bar K in the whitened geometry.
  2. Fit the base terms of the next section (star, shared script, token distance, fertility difference) by non-negative least squares.
  3. Take the residuals r_{ij} of this fit and add a constant so that every off-diagonal residual is non-negative.
  4. Apply classical multidimensional scaling to the residual matrix and keep the first two coordinates.
  5. Orient the first coordinate so that the mean of the object-before-verb languages is positive.

The method receives no information about family or word order. Only step 5 uses word order, and it only fixes the sign of the first coordinate.

Two features of the map guide the tests. First, the word-order groups cut across a family. Hindi, Urdu, Marathi and Persian are Indo-European and object-first; the Germanic, Romance and Slavic languages are not. Second, languages of one family stay close together inside each end. Apart from Indo-Iranian, the object-before-verb group consists of complete families (Turkic, Dravidian, Japanese, Korean). A test must therefore separate the two structures.
