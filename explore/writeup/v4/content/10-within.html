A word-order direction from inside each language

This section estimates a word-order direction from pairs of sentences that differ only in word order, inside each language. It shows three pre-registered results. The within-language direction is aligned with the between-language word-order direction in the middle layers, in Qwen2.5-7B and, in a pre-registered replication, in Llama-3.1-8B. In Qwen, steering along it reorders noun objects in German and Dutch, and unlike the between-language direction it keeps the model in the prompt language. In Llama the same pattern appears, but the pre-registered steering tests do not pass.

Why a second direction

The word-order direction \beta_{\text{OV}} is a difference between groups of languages. It therefore can carry part of what makes those languages different as languages. The free-generation studies show this. Pushed against their own order, Japanese, Turkish and Chinese (first study) and Polish and Croatian (confirmatory study) often left their language.

A direction estimated from pairs of sentences in the same language cannot carry language identity, because both sentences of a pair are in one language. We call it the within-language direction d_W. The study asks three questions, each pre-registered with a claim (commit 14fa22e):

  1. A, alignment. Does d_W point along \beta_{\text{OV}}?
  2. B, reordering. Does steering along d_W change the order of noun objects?
  3. C, retention. Does d_W keep the model in the prompt language better than \beta_{\text{OV}} does?

The within-language direction

We use the 999 minimal pairs of the preference test, in 8 languages. Each pair holds an attested sentence and its twin with the verb and its object swapped, with the same tokens. For each sentence we take the mean-pooled residual stream over the content tokens, at every layer. This is the same pooling as for the language centroids. For pair p, let

\Delta_p=\bar h\big(x^{\text{OV}}_p\big)-\bar h\big(x^{\text{VO}}_p\big).

The difference mixes two effects: the change of order, and the change from an attested sentence to a constructed one, which is less natural. If the attested order is VO, the OV twin is the constructed one; if it is OV, the VO twin is. We separate the two effects by weighted least squares, per layer and per coordinate:

\Delta_p=d_W+s_p\,d_U+e_p,\qquad s_p=\begin{cases}+1&\text{attested order VO}\\-1&\text{attested order OV}\end{cases},\qquad w_p=\frac{1}{n_{\ell(p)}} .

Here d_U takes the unnaturalness of the constructed twin, and d_W is the within-language word-order direction. The weight w_p divides by the number of pairs of the pair's language \ell(p), so that every language counts equally.

  • Validation. With planted order and unnaturalness vectors and the real pair counts, the estimator recovers both (cosine 0.998 and 1.000). A plain mean of the differences does not (cosine 0.92 with the order vector, 0.46 with the unnaturalness vector). At layer 14 the two estimated components have a cosine of 0.54, so a plain mean would mix them.
  • Held-out versions. For claim A we fit d_W on all 8 pair languages. For steering German, Russian and English, which are pair languages, we fit it without that language's pairs.
  • Reliability. The cosine between estimates from two halves of the pairs is 0.85 to 0.94 at layers 2 to 28, and 0.54 at layer 1.
  • Layer 0. The two twins contain the same tokens, so their mean-pooled embeddings are identical and d_W is zero up to rounding. Layer 0 carries no information in this study.
  • Norm. At layer 14 the natural norm of d_W is 1.24. For steering, we rescale it to \lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert of the prompt language, about 9.4 times larger, so that both directions have the same norm.

A. Alignment (pre-registered: holds)

At layer 14 we compare d_W with \beta_{\text{OV}} estimated from all 34 languages. The null distribution uses 10,000 random unit vectors, uniform in the 33-dimensional span of the 34 centred dev centroids. This is the space in which \beta_{\text{OV}} lies. The claim needs a positive cosine above the 99.9th percentile of the null.

The cosine is +0.232, against a 99.9th percentile of +0.206 (null standard deviation 0.071; p = 0.0002). The claim holds. Most of d_W lies outside the span of language differences: the span holds 16% of its squared norm. Inside the span, the cosine with \beta_{\text{OV}} is +0.58, and

\cos(d_W,\beta_{\text{OV}})=\sqrt{\tfrac{\lVert P d_W\rVert^2}{\lVert d_W\rVert^2}}\;\cos(P d_W,\beta_{\text{OV}})\approx\sqrt{0.16}\times0.58\approx0.23,

where P projects onto the span. The unnaturalness component is not aligned: \cos(d_U,\beta_{\text{OV}})={+0.003}.

The Indo-European control is not near zero. The pre-registration expected \cos(d_W,\beta_{\text{IE}}) near 0. It is −0.18 at layer 14, about 2.5 null standard deviations below zero, and between −0.15 and −0.24 at layers 6 to 18. This expectation therefore did not hold.

We give one possible reason, which we did not test. In our sample, OV order and Indo-European membership are negatively associated: 4 of 16 Indo-European languages are OV, against 7 of 18 others. In the held-out fits used for steering, \beta_{\text{OV}} and \beta_{\text{IE}} themselves are almost orthogonal (cosine −0.03 to +0.02). In Llama-3.1-8B the same cosine is near zero (next subsection). The Qwen value therefore does not replicate and looks specific to Qwen.

A in a second model (pre-registered: holds)

We repeated claim A in Llama-3.1-8B, a model trained independently of Qwen. We committed the pre-registration before any Llama pair states existed (commit 7bd19c0). The pairs, the pooling, the estimator and the null are those of the Qwen study. The vectors \beta_{\text{OV}} and \beta_{\text{IE}} come from the same regression on the 34 Llama dev centroids. The primary layer is 16 of 32, relative depth 0.5, the same depth as layer 14 of 28 in Qwen. This study only extracts states; we did not steer Llama.

  • Claim. The cosine of d_W with \beta_{\text{OV}} at layer 16 is +0.319, against a 99.9th percentile of +0.209 (null standard deviation 0.075). With p = 0.0001, the smallest value that 10,000 draws can give, the claim holds. The alignment is stronger than in Qwen.
  • Span. The language span holds 18.5% of the squared norm of d_W. Inside the span, the cosine with \beta_{\text{OV}} is +0.74, with a maximum of +0.76 at layer 17.
  • Profile. The cosine exceeds the 99.9th percentile of the null at layers 8 to 25. It is largest, +0.36, at layer 14 (relative depth 0.44). It is between +0.05 and +0.11 at layers 1 to 5 and between −0.02 and +0.09 at layers 26 to 31. At the last layer it is −0.22, close to the −0.20 of Qwen at its last layer.
  • Secondary results. Split-half reliability is 0.89 to 0.93 at layers 2 to 31. The unnaturalness component is not aligned (cosine −0.02). The Indo-European control is −0.07 at layer 16 (0.9 null standard deviations) and between −0.14 and −0.05 at layers 6 to 18, near zero.

In two models trained independently, the direction that separates object-first from verb-first sentences inside a language lies along the direction that separates object-first from verb-first languages, in the middle layers.

B. Reordering (pre-registered: holds)

We repeat the confirmatory free-generation run with d_W: the same 1,350 prompts in 9 languages, greedy decoding, steering at block 14 at every position. The conditions are unsteered; d_W at k\in\{\pm1,\pm2,\pm3\}; and \beta_{\text{OV}} at k=\pm2, run again for a paired comparison. The measure and the inclusion rule are those of the confirmatory study: noun-object share, and at least 20 noun-object pairs unsteered, at +2 and at −2.

  • Statistic. The pooled slope \bar b(d_W): the mean of b_i(d_W)=\big(\rho^{\text{N}}_i(+2d_W)-\rho^{\text{N}}_i(-2d_W)\big)/4 over the included languages of the flexible set (German, Dutch, Russian, Ukrainian, Polish, Croatian).
  • Null. The 24 random directions of the confirmatory study, pooled over the same set, each with its own inclusion.
  • Claim. At least 3 languages included for d_W, at least 20 random directions defined, \bar b(d_W) above every random pooled slope, and z>2.58.

The unsteered and \beta_{\text{OV}} continuations are 100% identical to those of the confirmatory study, because greedy decoding is deterministic here. The random null from that study is therefore exact for this run.

All 6 flexible languages are included. The pooled slope is +3.5 percentage points per unit of k. The 24 random directions give −0.1 ± 0.8, with a largest value of +1.7. The slope exceeds all of them, with z = 4.56 (empirical p = 1/25 = 0.04). The claim holds.

Two languages carry the effect: German (+10.3 points per unit of k) and Dutch (+8.5). The Slavic slopes are near zero: Croatian +1.5, Ukrainian +0.6, Russian +0.1 and Polish 0.0. In German and Dutch the change goes both ways:

At k=\pm1 the shares stay near the unsteered value (German 71% and 70%, Dutch 72% and 73%). The pooled slope is about zero at k = 1 (−0.05 points per unit of k), +3.5 at k = 2 and +5.1 at k = 3. In the Slavic languages d_W has no effect at k = 2, while \beta_{\text{OV}} moves Russian from 4% to 58%. At k = +3, a secondary condition, the Slavic shares start to rise: Croatian from 5% to 33%, Polish from 3% to 18%, Ukrainian from 3% to 11%. In the controls, the share stays at 0% to 2% in English and Spanish and at 100% in Korean, at every strength of d_W.

C. Language retention (pre-registered: holds)

For each of the 9 languages, the against sign of k pushes the language away from its own majority order. At the against sign and k = 2, we compare the share of continuations in the prompt language under d_W and under \beta_{\text{OV}}, in the same run. The claim needs claim B and a higher share for d_W in at least 8 of the 9 languages.

Under d_W, 98% of continuations stay in the prompt language on average, against 76% under \beta_{\text{OV}}. The share is higher for d_W in 8 of the 9 languages, and Dutch ties (99% under both). The sign test over the 8 languages that differ gives p = 0.008. The claim holds. The largest differences are in Polish (98% against 27%), Croatian (95% against 37%) and Korean (100% against 71%).

The text also degrades less. At k = +2, the share of repeated word bigrams is 5.2% in Polish and 9.3% in Croatian under d_W. Under \beta_{\text{OV}} it is 24.8% and 26.0%, and unsteered it is 3.8% and 5.3%.

All three pre-registered claims hold, and claim A replicates in a second model. The word-order direction that the model uses inside each language lies along the between-language word-order axis in the middle layers. The cosine is +0.23 in Qwen2.5-7B (p = 0.0002) and +0.32 in Llama-3.1-8B (p = 0.0001). In Qwen, steering along it reorders noun objects (pooled over six flexible-order languages, z = 4.56; the effect comes from German and Dutch). It also keeps the model in the prompt language (98% against 76%). In Llama, the same steering pattern appears, but neither pre-registered steering claim passes (next subsection).

Steering in a second model (pre-registered: not replicated)

We repeated both steering experiments in Llama-3.1-8B. We committed the pre-registration before any Llama generation (commit 2c1b420). The primary claims fail. The qualitative pattern of Qwen repeats, but neither pre-registered test passes.

  • Design. The 1,350 prompts, the measure, the inclusion rule and the units of k are those of the Qwen studies. Steering is at the output of block 16 of 32, relative depth 0.5.
  • Directions. \beta_{\text{OV}} and \beta_{\text{IE}} come from the 34 Llama dev centroids, each test language held out, and 24 random directions from their span. d_W comes from the Llama replication of claim A, held out for German, Russian and English, rescaled to \lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert.
  • Strength. Relative to the spread of the language centroids, \lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert is 0.80 in Llama and 0.73 in Qwen, so k = 2 is a comparable push.
  • Conditions. Unsteered; \beta_{\text{OV}} and d_W at k = ±1, ±2, ±3; 24 random directions at ±2. These 61 conditions give 82,350 continuations. The null uses the random directions of this run.
  • Hypotheses. L-H1 repeats H1 of the confirmatory study: \beta_{\text{OV}} over German and Russian. L-B repeats claim B: d_W over the six flexible languages. L-C repeats claim C and is tested only if L-B holds. The claim rules are those of the Qwen studies (above every random slope and z > 2.58; for L-C, at least 8 of 9 languages).

Verdict. L-H1 fails: the pooled slope is +4.4 points per unit of k, with z = 1.86. L-B fails: the pooled slope is +3.2 and exceeds all 24 random slopes, but z = 2.21 is below 2.58. L-C was therefore not tested.

L-H1, between-language direction (pre-registered: fails). Of the 24 random directions, 21 are defined. They give +0.2 ± 2.2 points per unit of k, with a largest value of +6.7 (second largest +4.6). The word-order slope of +4.4 is below the largest, so the empirical p is 3/22 = 0.14. In German the slope is +6.3, and the change goes both ways: 57%, 71% and 82% of noun objects come first at k = −2, 0 and +2. In Russian the slope is only +2.4: the share rises from 4% to 15% at k = +2, against 4% to 58% in Qwen.

L-B, within-language direction (pre-registered: fails on z). The 24 random directions give +0.0 ± 1.4 points per unit of k, with a largest value of +2.4. Their spread is about twice that in Qwen (0.8). The pattern is that of Qwen:

  • German (+7.4 per unit of k): 8%, 44%, 71%, 74% and 86% at k = −3, −2, 0, +2 and +3.
  • Dutch (+7.7): 23%, 60%, 76%, 91% and 89%.
  • The Slavic languages are near zero at k = 2 (Croatian +2.2, Russian +1.7, Ukrainian +0.4, Polish −0.5). At k = +3, Russian rises to 17.5%, Polish to 20% and Croatian to 19%; Ukrainian stays at 0%.

L-C, retention (not tested; descriptive). At the against sign and k = 2, d_W keeps 97% of continuations in the prompt language and \beta_{\text{OV}} 89%. d_W is higher in 6 of 9 languages; German and English tie, and Dutch is 97% against 98%. At every strength up to k = ±3, d_W keeps at least 91% of continuations in every language.

Secondary results (pre-registered, no claim).

  • Between-language direction over Dutch, Ukrainian, Polish and Croatian (the H2 set of the confirmatory study). The pooled slope is +9.8 points per unit of k, above all 24 random slopes (largest +2.9), z = 6.27. Ukrainian rises from 2% to 43%, Polish from 0% to 39% and Croatian from 3% to 30% at k = +2. At that strength, 74%, 91% and 59% of their continuations stay in the prompt language. Dutch falls from 76% to 47% at k = −2. In Qwen this set was not evaluable.
  • Strength 3. \beta_{\text{OV}} at k = +3 moves the model out of the language: only 0% to 27% of continuations stay in Russian, Ukrainian, Polish, Croatian and Dutch, and 44% in German. Over the flexible set, the pooled slope of \beta_{\text{OV}} is +1.6, +8.0 and −0.1 points per unit of k at k = 1, 2 and 3. For d_W it is +0.2, +3.2 and +5.4.

In Llama, as in Qwen, d_W reorders German and Dutch in both directions and keeps the language. Also as in Qwen, \beta_{\text{OV}} reorders languages with flexible order and, pushed harder, moves the model out of the language. But the pre-registered tests do not pass. Two differences explain this in part. The languages that respond to \beta_{\text{OV}} differ between the models: Russian in Qwen, and Ukrainian, Polish and Croatian in Llama. And the random directions vary about twice as much in Llama, which raises the bar for z.

How much of the between-language effect runs through the within-language direction? (exploratory)

The following argument uses arithmetic that we did after seeing the data. Both directions are scaled to \lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert. A push of k\,\beta_{\text{OV}} therefore contains a component along d_W equal to a push along d_W of strength

k_{\text{eff}}=k\,\cos(\beta_{\text{OV}},d_W),\qquad \text{component}=k\,\cos(\beta_{\text{OV}},d_W)\,\lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert .

In Qwen, per flexible language, the cosine of the steering d_W with \beta_{\text{OV}} is 0.20 to 0.23, so at k = 2 we get k_{\text{eff}} = 0.40 to 0.47. Even at the larger strength k = ±1, d_W has no clear effect. For example, German is at 71%, 66% and 70% at k = −1, 0 and +1, and Russian at 7%, 4% and 5%. Yet \beta_{\text{OV}} at k = −2 moves German to 8%, and at k = +2 Russian to 58%.

Assume that the effects of the components add without interaction; we did not test this. Then most of the effect of \beta_{\text{OV}} on word order runs through components orthogonal to d_W. This agrees with two exploratory observations. \beta_{\text{OV}} makes the model write German with English verb placement, and it moves Polish and Croatian out of the language. In contrast, d_W reorders inside the language.

What this means, and its limits

The between-language word-order axis contains the model's own within-language word-order feature, in the middle layers, in both models that we tested. In Qwen, steering along that feature reorders noun objects in German and Dutch without a change of language. In Llama the pattern is the same, but it does not reach the pre-registered standard. By the exploratory argument above, the extra Slavic reordering of \beta_{\text{OV}} and its language switching probably come from components that d_W does not share. Several limits apply:

  • All states are mean-pooled over the sentence, so d_W is a sentence-level summary, not a per-token feature.
  • For steering, d_W is about 9.4 times its natural norm at layer 14. The strengths that reorder are large for this direction.
  • The pairs come from 8 languages. Of the 6 flexible-order generation languages, only German and Russian have pairs.
  • The causal results meet the pre-registered standard in Qwen only. In Llama the qualitative pattern repeats, but the tests do not pass. We steered one layer per model (14 and 16). The alignment cosines are 0.23 and 0.32: most of d_W lies outside the span of language differences.
  • The Indo-European control was not near zero in Qwen. It was near zero in Llama, so the Qwen value looks model-specific, and we do not know its cause.
