The Word-Order Axis

How two multilingual language models arrange 34 languages internally: a family tree in the layers that read and write words, a word-order axis in the middle, and evidence that the model uses that axis.

AuthorsDadhichi, with Claude
ModelsQwen2.5-7B · Llama-3.1-8B
PublishedOctober 2026
Code & pre-registrationsgithub.com/Dadhichi/Language-Geometry

Summary

  • We read sentence representations of 34 languages out of the residual stream of Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B, using translations of the same sentences (FLORES+).
  • After removing what is explained by script, shared tokens and sentence length, the arrangement of the languages carries two separate structures: the Glottolog family tree, and a word-order axis. Each survives controlling for the other. All tests were pre-registered, and the result replicates in the second model.
  • The word-order axis is head direction, the bundle of object–verb order, postpositions and genitive order, with object–verb order its most distinctive part. The family tree is not explained by vocabulary the languages share.
  • The two structures sit at different depths. Word order peaks in the middle layers and fades before the output. The family tree is strongest in the first and last layers, where the model reads and writes words.
  • The word-order axis is used. Adding a direction estimated from other languages to the middle of the network shifts the model's preference between object-before-verb and verb-before-object versions of 999 real sentences, in all 8 languages tested (z = 4.99 against random directions of the same size).
  • At the token level, the model's sense of "which language is this" is graded when the evidence is ambiguous, but it switches within about one token when the language actually changes.

Languages as points

Interpretability work keeps finding that models give familiar objects a familiar shape. Days of the week and months sit on circles. Numbers lie on helices, and when a model tracks how many characters fit on a line, the count lives on a rippled curve that attention heads rotate to compare against the line width [1]. In each case the geometry mirrors the structure of the thing being represented.

Languages have structure too. Linguists organise them in two classic ways: by descent, in family trees such as Indo-European, and by type, along parameters such as whether the object comes before the verb ("the dog the ball chased") or after it ("the dog chased the ball"). A model trained on text from many languages is never told either fact. We ask whether either of these structures appears in how the model arranges languages internally, and if so, whether the model uses it.

Earlier work shows that multilingual representations separate into a shared, roughly language-neutral part and a language-specific part, and that subtracting each language's average representation improves cross-lingual matching [2][3]. Language centroids cluster loosely by family [3], and family trees can be recovered from models' output distributions [8]. What has been missing is a test that separates genealogy from typology and from surface overlap (shared script and vocabulary), done inside decoder LLMs, across depth, with a causal check. That is what this article does.

Setup

Models. Qwen2.5-7B (28 transformer blocks, residual width 3,584) and Llama-3.1-8B (32 blocks, width 4,096), both base models.

Text. FLORES+ [9] provides the same 997 + 1,012 sentences professionally translated into 200+ languages. We use 34 languages chosen to separate family, script and word order: 13 families, 12 scripts, 11 object-before-verb languages spread over five families, and three pairs of one language written in two scripts (Hindi/Urdu, Croatian/Serbian, Simplified/Traditional Chinese).

Representations

Write h^\ell_t(x)\in\mathbb R^d for the residual stream at position t of input x=(x_0,x_1,\dots,x_T), where \ell=0 is the embedding output and \ell=1,\dots,L the output of block \ell. Every input begins with the same sink token x_0 (BOS for Llama, a newline for Qwen, which has no BOS). The residual stream at position 0 has an unusually large norm in the middle layers, so it is excluded, and a sentence is represented by the mean over its content positions:

\bar h^\ell(x)=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}h^\ell_t(x).

For language i with sentences s\in\mathcal S, the centroid is \mu_i^\ell=\frac1{|\mathcal S|}\sum_{s}\bar h^\ell(x_{i,s}), and its offset is a_i^\ell=\mu_i^\ell-\frac1N\sum_{j}\mu_j^\ell with N=34. Every statement below concerns how the 34 offsets sit relative to one another.

Two geometries

An inner product \langle u,v\rangle_M=u^\top Mv needs a choice of M. We use two.

M_{\text{causal}}=\operatorname{diag}(g)\,\operatorname{Cov}_{y}\!\big[\gamma(y)\big]\,\operatorname{diag}(g),\qquad M_{\text{wh}}=\Big((1-\alpha)\,W+\alpha\,\tfrac{\operatorname{tr}W}{d}\,I\Big)^{-1},\ \ \alpha=\tfrac12.

In M_{\text{causal}}, \gamma(y) is the unembedding vector of token y, the covariance runs over the real vocabulary (151,665 tokens for Qwen, the 128,000 non-special tokens for Llama), and g is the gain of the final RMSNorm. This is the causal inner product of Park et al. [5], under which a direction's length measures how much it moves the logits; we apply it at every layer as a logit-lens proxy. In M_{\text{wh}}, W is the pooled within-language covariance of sentence representations, so distances are measured in units of how much sentences of one language vary. The shrinkage \alpha keeps the inverse stable in 3,584 or 4,096 dimensions.

Unbiased distances

A distance computed from estimated centroids is biased upward: \mathbb E\lVert\hat a_i-\hat a_j\rVert_M^2=\lVert a_i-a_j\rVert_M^2+\operatorname{tr}\!\big(M(\Sigma_i+\Sigma_j)\big)/n, so languages whose sentences vary more look farther apart. FLORES comes in two disjoint halves (dev and devtest), so we estimate every offset twice, \hat a^{(1)} and \hat a^{(2)}, and cross them:

K_{ij}=\tfrac12\big(\hat a_i^{(1)\top}M\,\hat a_j^{(2)}+\hat a_j^{(1)\top}M\,\hat a_i^{(2)}\big),\qquad D^2_{ij}=K_{ii}+K_{jj}-2K_{ij}.

Because the two halves contain different sentences, their sampling errors are independent and \mathbb E[K_{ij}]=a_i^\top Ma_j exactly, so D^2 is unbiased. The primary statistics average trace-normalised Grams over a pre-registered window of middle layers \mathcal W (layers 8–20 of 28 for Qwen, 9–23 of 32 for Llama, the same relative depth):

\bar K=\frac1{|\mathcal W|}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal W}\frac{K^\ell}{\operatorname{tr}K^\ell}.

Sentence-bootstrap 90% intervals on every headline statistic below are about ±0.001; sampling noise in the centroids is not a limiting factor. The number of languages is.

A first look: translations and a trap

A pilot on 12 languages gave a simple picture of the middle layers. To a good approximation,

\bar h^{\ell}(x_{i,s})\;\approx\;m+a_i+g_i\,b_s+\varepsilon_{i,s},

where m is a common mean, a_i a constant offset for language i, b_s the content of sentence s shared by all its translations, g_i a scalar gain, and \varepsilon what is left. The offset term carries most of the language difference: subtracting each language's centroid and matching by cosine similarity, \hat s=\arg\max_{s'}\cos\big(\bar h(x_{i,s})-\mu_i,\ \bar h(x_{j,s'})-\mu_j\big), finds the correct translation among a thousand candidates 99.1% of the time at layer 14 of Qwen. A translation alone removes 82–91% of the cross-language error that any linear map can remove; the remaining rotations are small (2–4% of variance in the middle layers).

The gain g_i turned out to be a property of the pooling, not of the model. With n_i the mean number of tokens per sentence in language i,

\log g_i\;\approx\;c-\tfrac12\log n_i\qquad(\text{correlation }{-0.93}\text{ to }{-0.96}\text{ at every layer}).

This is the scaling of an average of weakly correlated vectors: if the component of each token state that carries the sentence's content varies from token to token with little correlation, the norm of its mean over n tokens falls like n^{-1/2}. Rescaling sentence vectors by \sqrt{n} removes 80% of the variance in \log g_i. Every analysis below includes \lvert\log n_i-\log n_j\rvert as a covariate and reports a \sqrt n-rescaled variant.

A trap: maps that seem not to compose

The pilot also produced a cautionary result. We first fit orthogonal maps between languages in each language's own top-k principal subspace. With B_i\in\mathbb R^{d\times k} the top-k principal directions of language i and Z_i=(X_i-\mu_i)B_i,

R_{ij}=\arg\min_{R\in O(k)}\lVert Z_iR-Z_j\rVert_F,\qquad c_{ijm}=\frac{\lVert Z_i\,(R_{ij}R_{jm}-R_{im})\rVert_F^2}{\lVert Z_i\rVert_F^2}.

If languages were related by a single shared representation, the maps would compose and c_{ijm}\approx0. Measured against a null built that way, the real c was 1.5–4.5 times too large. The cause is the truncation. Suppose the languages are exactly consistent, X_j-\mu_j=(X_i-\mu_i)\,Q_{ij} with orthogonal Q_{ij}Q_{jm}=Q_{im}. The best k\times k map is close to the orthogonal part of T_{ij}=B_i^\top Q_{ij}B_j, and

T_{ij}\,T_{jm}=B_i^\top Q_{ij}\,P_j\,Q_{jm}B_m,\qquad P_j=B_jB_j^\top,

which equals T_{im}=B_i^\top Q_{im}B_m only when the projector P_j onto language j's subspace acts as the identity on the directions involved, that is, only when the three subspaces are carried exactly onto one another. Estimated from a thousand sentences they are not (the overlap of top-64 subspaces between two halves of the data is 0.4–0.6). So the composition test was measuring subspace mismatch. Four independent analyses confirmed this: perfectly consistent synthetic data run through the same pipeline gave c=0.091 against 0.077 real (the original null predicted 0.017), and choosing one shared subspace for all languages (B_j:=Q_{ij}^\top B_i) made the real maps compose as well as consistent synthetic data did (0.0016 against 0.0020). It is an easy mistake with per-language PCA, and it is why the rest of the article works with distances between centroids, which need no per-language basis.

The residual map

Most of the variation in distances between centroids is a "star": each language sits at its own distance from the centre, mostly set by tokens per sentence, with long-tokenised languages like Telugu and Khmer far out. We fit that star, plus terms for shared script, shared tokens and the token-count difference, and look at what is left. Figure 1 lays the 34 languages out by those residual distances (classical multidimensional scaling), without telling the method anything about families or word order.

Testing for a tree and an axis

A picture is not a test. To test whether the distances carry a family tree or a word-order split, we start from the standard model of evolution along a tree. Suppose each language's offset is a sum of independent random steps along the branches from a root:

a_i=\sum_{e\,\in\,\mathrm{path}(r,\,i)}\xi_e,\qquad \mathbb E[\xi_e]=0,\quad \mathbb E\big[\xi_e^\top M\xi_e\big]=\sigma_e^2,\quad \xi_e\ \text{independent}.

Steps on the shared part of two languages' paths cancel in their difference, and the rest add in quadrature:

\mathbb E\,\lVert a_i-a_j\rVert_M^2=\sum_{e\,\in\,\mathrm{path}(i,\,j)}\sigma_e^2 .

Every internal branch e splits the languages into those below it and the rest, a split S_e, and it contributes \sigma_e^2 to exactly the pairs that the split separates. The leaf branches contribute a per-language term. Writing \delta_S(i,j)=\mathbb 1\big[\lvert S\cap\{i,j\}\rvert=1\big], a tree therefore predicts

D^2_{ij}=\ell_i+\ell_j+\sum_{S\in\mathcal S}b_S\,\delta_S(i,j)+\beta^\top c_{ij}+\epsilon_{ij},\qquad \ell,\ b,\ \beta\ \ge 0 .

The per-language terms \ell_i form the "star" (each language's own distance from the centre). The covariates c_{ij} remove known surface effects: indicators for the four scripts shared by more than one language (Latin, Cyrillic, Arabic, Devanagari), the token distance 1-\sum_y\min\big(p_i(y),p_j(y)\big) between the languages' token-frequency distributions on the FLORES text, and the length difference \lvert\log n_i-\log n_j\rvert. All coefficients are constrained to be non-negative and the model is fit by non-negative least squares over the 561 language pairs. A set of splits \mathcal S is scored by its gain, the fraction of the base model's residual error it removes:

G(\mathcal S)=1-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}\big(\text{base}\cup\mathcal S\big)}{\mathrm{SSE}\big(\text{base}\big)} .

We test two structures, each against a permutation null that keeps everything about the structure except its alignment with the languages:

  • Genealogy, \mathcal S_{\text{tree}}: the 20 internal splits of the Glottolog tree [11] restricted to our languages (Indo-European, Germanic, West Germanic, Romance, Ibero-Romance, Slavic, East and South Slavic, Indo-Iranian, Indo-Aryan, Semitic, Turkic, Finnic, and the two-language families). The null relabels the tree's leaves at random, keeping its shape.
  • Word order, S_{\text{OV}}: one split, the 11 object-before-verb languages of WALS 83A (Kazakh, uncoded, assigned by its genus) against the rest. The null draws random sets of 11 languages.

With N=10^4 permutations \pi, p=\big(1+\#\{\pi:G(\pi\mathcal S)\ge G(\mathcal S)\}\big)/(1+N), so the smallest attainable value is about 0.0001.

The two structures overlap, because the object-before-verb group consists largely of whole families (Indo-Aryan, Turkic, Dravidian, Japanese, Korean). Each claim therefore had to hold conditionally as well: genealogy with the word-order split already in the base, G(\mathcal S_{\text{tree}}\mid S_{\text{OV}}), and word order with all 20 family splits already in the base, G(S_{\text{OV}}\mid\mathcal S_{\text{tree}}). We verified this design on simulated distances before any data existed. The simulation used Brownian motion on the real tree, a realistic star including one high-fertility outlier, nuisance script structure, and 34 languages:

Results

Both claims hold in both geometries and both models. Bonferroni-corrected threshold 0.0125; 0.0001 is the smallest p-value 10,000 permutations can produce. The table also reports the same-language/two-script test: Hindi–Urdu, Croatian–Serbian and the two Chinese scripts are closer than script, tokens and length predict, among the closest 1% of all 561 language pairs in the Mahalanobis geometry of both models.

Exploratory checks, run after these results, did not change them. Adding geographic distance between the languages leaves both effects significant. Dropping each of the 11 families in turn leaves the word-order effect significant in all 24 runs and the genealogy effect significant in all of them, weakest when all of Indo-European is removed (18 languages left, p = 0.046). Adding German and Dutch, which put the verb second in main clauses, to the object-before-verb group weakens the effect, so what aligns with the geometry is main-clause object–verb order.

Where in depth

Repeating the test layer by layer shows that the two structures live in different places.

This is the depth profile one would expect if the two structures had different sources. Languages of a family share vocabulary, so a genealogical signal at the embedding and in the output-preparing layers could simply reflect which tokens a language uses. Word order is not visible in any single token; it is a property of how tokens are arranged, and it appears exactly in the layers that earlier work describes as the model's language-neutral "concept space" [4].

Which parameter, and is it vocabulary?

A bundle of word-order features

The word-order split was defined by a single feature, the order of object and verb. But word-order features travel together. Languages that put the object before the verb usually also put the preposition after its noun ("the house in", called a postposition) and the possessor before the possessed ("the house's roof"). Linguists describe this as head direction: whether the head of a phrase comes first or last. So the axis could encode verb–object order specifically, or the whole bundle.

Three of our languages break the bundle: Persian puts the object before the verb but uses prepositions, while Finnish and Estonian put the verb first but use postpositions. That is few, but it is enough to ask whether either feature explains the geometry beyond the other, with the family tree held fixed. We define four splits from WALS (uncoded languages assigned by genus):

Splits

S_{\text{OV}} (83A, object before verb): hin urd mar pes tur azj kaz jpn kor tam tel

S_{\text{POST}} (85A, postpositions): hin urd mar tur azj kaz fin ekk jpn kor tam tel

S_{\text{GEN}} (86A, genitive before noun): swe hin urd mar tur azj kaz fin ekk cmn ×2 jpn kor tam tel

S_{\text{NADJ}} (87A, noun before adjective): fra spa por pes arb heb mlt ind vie khm

The pre-registered tests are the two conditional gains G\big(S_{\text{OV}}\mid\mathcal S_{\text{tree}}\cup\{S_{\text{POST}}\}\big) and G\big(S_{\text{POST}}\mid\mathcal S_{\text{tree}}\cup\{S_{\text{OV}}\}\big), each against random splits of the same size, with the reading fixed in advance: if only the first survived, the axis would be specifically about verbs and objects; if both survived, it would be the bundle. As a description we also report each feature's gain alone, G(S_f\mid\mathcal S_{\text{tree}}), and its unique gain beyond the other three, G\big(S_f\mid\mathcal S_{\text{tree}}\cup\{S_{f'}:f'\ne f\}\big).

The pre-registered verdict is the bundle. Object–verb order explains the geometry beyond adposition order, and adposition order beyond object–verb order, in three of the four analyses and in both models (all p ≤ 0.0002; the exception is object–verb order in Qwen's causal geometry, p = 0.023). The axis is therefore best described as head direction, the shared parameter that linguists have long argued links these features [10], with object–verb order its most distinctive part. The steering experiments below change the order of verbs and objects, which is the part of the bundle they can test.

Genealogy is not just shared words

The family signal peaks where the model handles individual words, so it could reflect shared vocabulary: German Hand and English hand look alike to a tokenizer. Our base model already controls for token overlap. We added two stronger, pre-registered lexical distances as covariates. The first measures shared character sequences in the actual FLORES sentences after romanising every script, which captures cognates, loanwords and names as the model sees them:

\mathrm{LEX}^{\text{text}}_{ij}=1-\frac1{|\mathcal S|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}J\Big(G_3\big(\rho(x_{i,s})\big),\,G_3\big(\rho(x_{j,s})\big)\Big),

where \rho transliterates to lower-case Latin letters, G_3 is the set of character trigrams and J is the Jaccard index. The second is the ASJP distance between 40-item basic-vocabulary lists [13], the normalised Levenshtein distance between words for the same concept, divided by the same quantity for different concepts to remove chance similarity from shared sound inventories:

\mathrm{LDN}(u,v)=\frac{\mathrm{lev}(u,v)}{\max(\lvert u\rvert,\lvert v\rvert)},\qquad \mathrm{LDND}_{ij}=\frac{\operatorname{mean}_{c}\ \mathrm{LDN}\big(w_{ic},w_{jc}\big)}{\operatorname{mean}_{c\ne c'}\ \mathrm{LDN}\big(w_{ic},w_{jc'}\big)}.

The ASJP control is deliberately over-conservative: basic vocabulary is how linguists establish families in the first place, so partialling it out removes much of genealogy itself. The test is the genealogy gain with the lexical distances added to the base, against the same leaf-relabelling null.

Character overlap explains nothing beyond token overlap: the genealogy gain does not move. Basic vocabulary absorbs between almost none and about half of it, and what remains is still significant in every case. The family structure in the geometry is therefore not a by-product of the words that languages share in this text.

Is the axis used?

A direction can describe the data without the model ever reading it. To test use, we push the model along the axis and look for a change in behaviour that only word order could explain.

The direction. At layer 14 of Qwen we regress the offsets on language labels, one regression with all 3,584 coordinates as responses:

a_i=\beta_0+\beta_{\text{OV}}\,\mathbb 1_{\text{OV}}(i)+\beta_{\text{IE}}\,\mathbb 1_{\text{IE}}(i)+\sum_{\sigma\in\{\text{Latn},\text{Cyrl},\text{Arab},\text{Deva}\}}\beta_\sigma\,\mathbb 1_\sigma(i)+\beta_n\log n_i+e_i .

The coefficient \beta_{\text{OV}}\in\mathbb R^{3584} is a direction in the residual stream: how object-before-verb languages differ from the rest with family, script and length held fixed. For each test language h we re-estimate it by ordinary least squares on the other 33 languages, \beta_{\text{OV}}^{(-h)}, so the direction is never fit on the language it is applied to. As a first check, the held-out projection

\pi_h=\frac{\big\langle a_h,\ \beta^{(-h)}_{\text{OV}}\big\rangle}{\lVert\beta^{(-h)}_{\text{OV}}\rVert^2}

measures where language h falls on an axis whose unit is the object-before-verb contrast itself. Before any intervention, the left-out languages already fall on the correct side (Figure 3).

The test. From Universal Dependencies treebanks [12] in eight languages we took 999 real sentences in which a verb and its object are next to each other, and built a twin of each with the two swapped. Both versions use exactly the same tokens.

The intervention. We add a multiple of the held-out direction to the output of block 14 at every position, including the sink, h^{14}_t\leftarrow h^{14}_t+k\,\beta^{(-h)}_{\text{OV}} with k\in\{\pm1,\pm2\}, and score both twins of each pair p under the modified model:

m_k(p)=\log P_k\big(x^{\text{OV}}_p\big)-\log P_k\big(x^{\text{VO}}_p\big),\qquad \log P(x)=\sum_{t\ge1}\log P(x_t\mid x_{<t}),\qquad \Delta_k(p)=m_k(p)-m_0(p).

Because the twins contain exactly the same tokens, anything that shifts the probability of all text in a language, or of particular words, cancels in m; only a change in the preferred order survives. The pre-registered statistic is the dose–response slope, averaged over the eight languages with equal weight,

b=\frac1{|\mathcal L|}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal L}\frac{\sum_{k=\pm2}k\,\bar\Delta^{(\ell)}_k}{\sum_{k=\pm2}k^2},

where \bar\Delta^{(\ell)}_k is the mean over language \ell's pairs. Any push of this size disturbs the model somewhat, so the null is a set of 32 random directions of the same norm drawn from the same space of language differences: r=\lVert\beta_{\text{OV}}\rVert\,U\epsilon/\lVert U\epsilon\rVert, with U an orthonormal basis of the span of the 33 centred offsets and \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I). The claim required b_{\text{OV}} to exceed all 32 random slopes and z=(b_{\text{OV}}-\overline{b_r})/\mathrm{sd}(b_r)>2.33.

The effect appears in all eight languages, in both directions. Pushing a verb-first language toward object-first makes the object-first twin much more likely; pushing an object-first language toward verb-first does the reverse (Figure 5). Pushing a language further toward the order it already has does little, which is what one would expect if the model reads this direction to decide word order and is already confident in the familiar case.

The intervention is not surgical. At k = +2 it lowers the log-probability of the original sentence by about 34 nats on average, against 17 for random directions of the same size, and the preferred twin flips in only about 10% of pairs. The pre-registered comparison accounts for disruption, since random directions disrupt the model too without producing the effect. Still, these are shifts in preference between two given strings, and the next section asks whether they show up in what the model writes.

Language identity, token by token

So far each sentence was one point. A sequence model reads one token at a time, and a natural hypothesis, borrowed from work showing that transformers linearly represent belief states over the hidden variables of the process that generated their training data [6], is that the model keeps a probability distribution over which language it is reading and updates it with each token. Mean-pooled sentences hide this, so we recorded the residual stream at every token of the 12 pilot languages and estimated the model's own belief from language-conditioned likelihoods. With \tau_\ell the prompt "The following text is written in ℓ.",

\pi_t(\ell)\ \propto\ \exp\Big(\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\log P\big(x_\tau\mid\tau_\ell,\,x_{<\tau}\big)\Big),

a posterior over the 12 languages under a uniform prior. Each token's representation is reduced to its coordinates in the 11-dimensional span of the language offsets, z_t=S^\top h^\ell_t.

Graded belief (supported). If the representation encodes belief, an affine map from the posterior, z_t\approx W\pi_t+c, should predict it better than an affine map from the hard decision e_{\arg\max\pi_t}. On held-out sentences, at positions where the evidence is ambiguous (\max_\ell\pi_t(\ell)<0.9, 26,435 positions), it does, at every layer (p = 0.0001). The posterior also adds information beyond the identity and position of the current token: adding it to a token-plus-position model lowers the error at every layer (at layer 14 from 102 to 85).

Bayesian switching (not supported). We built 28,800 code-switched sequences: the first part of a sentence in language A followed by the rest of the same sentence in language B. Each token's position on the line between the two languages' centroids is

\lambda_t=\frac{(c_B-c_A)^\top(z_t-c_A)}{\lVert c_B-c_A\rVert^2},

0 at A's centroid and 1 at B's. A whole-sequence Bayesian observer predicts \lambda_t\approx\sigma(\mathrm{LLR}_t) with \mathrm{LLR}_t=\sum_{\tau\le t}\big[\log P(x_\tau\mid\tau_B,x_{<\tau})-\log P(x_\tau\mid\tau_A,x_{<\tau})\big], which rises slowly because the evidence for A accumulated before the switch has to be overcome. A step function, \lambda_t\approx a+b\,\mathbb 1[t\ge t_{\text{switch}}], predicts an immediate jump. The step won by a wide margin (held-out error 0.11 against 0.25). The representation does not drift the way accumulated evidence would. It jumps.

The representation therefore behaves like an estimate of the language of the current stretch of text, graded when that stretch is ambiguous, with a little inertia: the number of tokens until \lambda_t>0.5 grows with the length of the preceding passage in A (Spearman ρ = +0.13, p = 0.0005). In an exploratory follow-up we let the observer forget, L_h(t)=\sum_{\tau\le t}\rho^{\,t-\tau}d_\tau with \rho=2^{-1/h}, and fit the half-life h. The best fit (h=8 tokens) still did far worse than the step (0.21 against 0.11). A caveat applies to both tests: the prompt-based posterior is a noisy estimate of the model's belief. The true language label predicts the representation better than \pi_t does, so the internal state is more certain than our estimate of it.

What did not hold up

Several elegant hypotheses failed, and the failures constrain the picture. We list them because a structure search is only as informative as the structures it rules out.

HypothesisWhat we found
Languages are positions on a torus and the maps between them are rotations, as in rotary position embeddingsThe maps between languages are mostly a translation. The small rotations that remain do not commute and do not share planes.
The failure of maps to compose has low-dimensional structure (curvature tied to script or family)It was the per-language PCA artefact described above; with a shared subspace the maps compose.
Language centroids are sums of a script vector and a family vectorLeave-one-language-out prediction from script and family is worse than predicting the mean (R² −0.27 to −0.44).
Language offsets rotate into the unembedding subspace in late layersThey are never more aligned with the unembedding than the sentence content itself.
A family tree in 12 languagesToo few languages to test (power 18–42% in simulation). This is why the main study used 34.

Discussion and limitations

Two linguistically meaningful structures are present in how these models arrange languages, and they occupy different depths: genealogy at the layers that handle words, word order in the middle. Word order is the more interesting of the two, because it cannot be read off any single token, and because steering along it changes the model's word-order preferences in eight languages from five families.

Several limits apply. We have shown that the structure is present in two independently trained models, not how it emerges during training; models with released checkpoints would answer that. Only three of our languages break the head-direction bundle, so separating its parts rests on few data points. The intervention is blunt, and our steering direction was defined by object–verb order alone. All representations are averages over FLORES sentences, a single domain. We did not control for how much training data each language had, although tokens per sentence partly tracks it. And we tested four word-order features out of the hundreds WALS describes.

The broader point is methodological as much as linguistic. Geometric claims about language representations are easy to fool: per-language PCA invents inconsistency, mean pooling invents per-language scale, and shared tokens can masquerade as genealogy. Each finding above survived a test designed in advance to rule out such artefacts, and two of our own early findings did not.

Methods and pre-registration

Every confirmatory test was written, validated on simulated data, and committed before the data it used were extracted or analysed. Exploratory analyses are labelled as such in the repository.

Extraction

Residual streams are captured with forward hooks on the embedding module and on every decoder block (the last hidden state returned by Hugging Face already includes the final norm, so it is not used). Pooling happens inside each hook, so no full sequence of activations is kept across layers. Batches are formed by length and capped at a fixed number of padded tokens, with an automatic split on out-of-memory. Activations are stored in float16. Models run in bfloat16 with no quantisation. FLORES+ names Chinese \texttt{cmn\_Hans}/\texttt{cmn\_Hant}, Estonian \texttt{ekk} and Filipino \texttt{fil}.

Statistical details

All split models are fit by non-negative least squares on the 561 values of D^2_{ij}; covariate columns are divided by their standard deviation. The primary family of tests ({genealogy, word order} × {causal, whitened}) uses a Bonferroni threshold of 0.0125. Power was estimated before data collection by simulating Brownian motion on the Glottolog tree with realistic leaf lengths and script structure: with 34 languages and internal branches carrying 10% of the distance, the genealogy test has 80–86% power, against 18–42% for the 12-language pilot. Sentence-bootstrap intervals (50 resamples, the same sentence indices in every language) are reported for every primary gain. The \sqrt n-rescaled variant multiplies each sentence vector by \sqrt{n_{i,s}/\bar n} before averaging.

Robustness (exploratory)

Run after the primary results and labelled as exploratory in the repository: great-circle geographic distance between WALS coordinates as an extra covariate; leave-one-family-out for all 11 families; adding German and Dutch to the object-before-verb split; per-layer profiles in four geometries (Euclidean, two shrinkage levels, causal); steering flip rates and the change in log-probability of the attested sentence; a forgetting Bayesian observer for code switches.

Reproduction

Each study directory under \texttt{explore/} contains its pre-registration, frozen analysis code, the Colab job script and the result files. Extraction for one model and 34 languages takes about 30 minutes on an L4 GPU; the whole study used about 10 L4 GPU-hours.
